# Chapter 58: Building Your Investment Plan

## Overview

You've learned about markets, securities, accounts, strategies, and psychology. Now it's time to put it all together into a coherent plan that works for your specific situation. A written investment plan provides structure, keeps emotions in check, and dramatically improves your odds of success. This chapter walks you through creating your personalized investment plan.

---

## Why You Need a Written Plan

### The Problem Without a Plan

**Without a plan, you'll:**
- Make decisions based on emotion
- Chase whatever is working now
- Change strategies constantly
- Have no framework for evaluating success
- Likely underperform the market

**Studies show:** Investors without written plans earn significantly less than those with documented strategies.

### What a Plan Provides

**Structure:**
- Clear rules for what to do
- Removes real-time decision-making
- Consistent execution

**Discipline:**
- Harder to break written rules
- Accountability to yourself
- Framework for saying "no"

**Measurement:**
- Clear benchmarks for success
- Way to evaluate if strategy is working
- Basis for improvement

## Step 1: Define Your Goals

### What Are You Investing For?

**Be specific. Not "make money" but:**
- "Retirement at 55 with $2,000,000"
- "Down payment for house in 5 years: $80,000"
- "Children's college: $200,000 in 15 years"
- "Financial independence: $1,500,000 by age 50"

### Goal Characteristics

**Each goal should have:**

| Element | Example |
|---------|---------|
| Specific amount | $1,000,000 |
| Target date | January 2040 |
| Purpose | Retirement income |
| Priority level | Primary |
| Flexibility | Some (can work part-time) |

### Multiple Goals Framework

**Rank your goals:**

1. **Essential:** Must happen (basic retirement, emergency fund)
2. **Important:** Strong desire (kids' college, house upgrade)
3. **Aspirational:** Would be nice (early retirement, vacation home)

**Allocate accordingly:** Essential goals get conservative treatment. Aspirational goals can accept more risk.

### Goal Setting Example

**Sarah, 35:**
- **Goal 1 (Essential):** Retirement at 65 with $1,500,000 – Need $500/month
- **Goal 2 (Important):** Kids' college in 13 years – Need $150,000 – Currently $0
- **Goal 3 (Aspirational):** Early retirement at 55 – Need $2,000,000

## Step 3: Determine Your Risk Profile

### Risk Tolerance Assessment

**How would you feel if your portfolio dropped 30%?**
- A) Panic, probably sell everything
- B) Uncomfortable, might sell some
- C) Concerned, but would hold
- D) See it as buying opportunity
- E) Wouldn't notice/care

**Have you experienced a real market crash?**
- Never
- Yes, but with small money
- Yes, with significant money

**What's your investment timeline?**
- Less than 5 years
- 5-10 years
- 10-20 years
- More than 20 years

### Risk Capacity Assessment

**Can you afford to take risk?**

| Factor | Low Capacity | High Capacity |
|--------|--------------|---------------|
| Time horizon | Short (<5 years) | Long (>15 years) |
| Income stability | Unstable/variable | Stable/secure |
| Other resources | None | Pension, inheritance |
| Expenses | High, inflexible | Low, flexible |
| Dependents | Many | None/few |

### Setting Your Risk Level

**Combine tolerance and capacity:**

| | Low Capacity | High Capacity |
|---|---|---|
| **Low Tolerance** | Conservative | Moderate |
| **High Tolerance** | Moderate | Aggressive |

**When they conflict, lean toward the lower risk option.**

## Step 4: Choose Your Strategy

### Core Strategy Selection

**Based on previous chapters, select your primary approach:**

**Passive indexing:**
- Buy broad market index funds
- Minimal trading
- Focus on asset allocation
- Best for: Most investors

**Active stock selection:**
- Research individual companies
- Build concentrated portfolio
- Requires significant time/skill
- Best for: Dedicated investors with edge

**Income investing:**
- Focus on dividends and interest
- Emphasis on current income
- More conservative
- Best for: Retirees, income-focused

**Trading:**
- Active buying and selling
- Short-term focus
- Requires time, skill, discipline
- Best for: Very few people (be honest)

### Be Realistic About Time

**Hours per week available for investing:**
- Less than 1 hour: Passive indexing only
- 1-3 hours: Passive with occasional stock picks
- 5-10 hours: Active management possible
- 10+ hours: Trading possible (but still hard)

### Strategy Documentation

**Write down:**
- My primary strategy is: _____
- I chose this because: _____
- I will spend _____ hours per week on investing
- I expect to make _____ trades per year

## Step 7: Define Your Rules

### Contribution Rules

- I will contribute $_____ per month to investing
- Contributions happen on: _____ (day of month)
- Contributions are automatic: Yes/No
- I will increase contributions by ____% annually

### Rebalancing Rules

- I will check allocation: _____ (monthly/quarterly/annually)
- I will rebalance when any asset class drifts _____% from target
- Rebalancing method: _____ (sell/buy, new contributions, or both)

### Buying Rules

**For passive investors:**
- I buy on schedule regardless of market conditions
- I invest lump sums immediately (or DCA over _____ months)

**For active investors:**
- I only buy when these conditions are met: _____
- Maximum position size: _____% of portfolio
- I wait _____ days between buy decisions

### Selling Rules

**For passive investors:**
- I only sell for rebalancing
- I only sell when I need the money
- I don't sell based on market conditions

**For active investors:**
- I sell when: _____
- Stop-loss level: _____% below entry
- I take profits at: _____

### Emergency Rules

- If market drops _____%, I will: _____ (nothing/rebalance/buy more)
- If I lose my job, I will: _____
- If I need cash unexpectedly, I will tap: _____ (order of accounts)

## Step 8: Define Success Metrics

### How You'll Measure Success

**Annual review metrics:**

| Metric | Target | Actual |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Savings rate | 20% of gross | |
| Portfolio return | Match benchmark ±2% | |
| Rule compliance | 90%+ | |
| Progress toward goals | On track | |

### Benchmarks

**Compare performance to:**
- Your own allocation (not S&P 500 if you own bonds)
- A target-date fund with similar allocation
- Simple 60/40 or 80/20 portfolio

**Don't compare:**
- Your balanced portfolio to the S&P 500
- Your returns to day traders on social media
- Yourself to market highs in hindsight

## Putting It All Together: Sample Plan

### Executive Summary

**Name:** Alex Sample
**Age:** 32
**Goal:** Retire at 60 with $2,000,000

### Investment Strategy

**Primary approach:** Passive index investing with annual rebalancing

**Target allocation:**
- 80% stocks (50% US, 20% international developed, 10% emerging)
- 15% bonds (all US investment grade)
- 5% REITs

### Account Strategy

| Account | Annual Contribution | Investments |
|---------|---------------------|-------------|
| 401(k) | $23,000 | US Stock Index, Bond Index |
| Roth IRA | $7,000 | International Stocks, REITs |
| Taxable | $6,000 | US Stock Index (VTI) |

### Rules

**Contributions:**
- $3,000/month total, automatic on the 1st
- Increase by 2% annually or with raises

**Rebalancing:**
- Review quarterly
- Rebalance if any asset drifts 5%+ from target
- Use new contributions first

**Buying/Selling:**
- No market timing
- No selling based on news or emotions
- Only sell for rebalancing or spending needs

### Review Schedule

- Quarterly: 15-minute allocation check
- Annually: Full 2-hour plan review in January
- Every 5 years: Major strategy review

### Emergency Provisions

- 6-month emergency fund maintained separately
- If unemployed: Reduce contributions to 401(k) match only
- If market drops 30%: Rebalance into stocks (don't sell)

## Common Planning Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Making It Too Complicated

**Keep it simple:**
- 3-5 funds is enough
- Simple rules beat complex ones
- If you can't explain it, simplify it

### 2. Being Too Ambitious

**Be realistic about:**
- Your time commitment
- Your ability to pick stocks
- Your ability to time markets
- Your emotional discipline

### 3. Not Writing It Down

**Written plans work better:**
- Easier to follow
- Harder to rationalize breaking
- Can review and improve

### 4. Never Reviewing

**Plans need maintenance:**
- Life changes
- Markets change
- You learn more
- Annual review is essential

### 5. Abandoning at the First Test

**Expect challenges:**
- Market drops happen
- Underperformance periods happen
- Stick to the plan unless fundamentally broken

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Write your plan down** - Documented plans dramatically outperform mental plans
- **Start with goals** - Know what you're investing for and when you need it
- **Be honest about risk** - Consider both tolerance and capacity
- **Match strategy to time** - Don't pick active trading if you have 2 hours weekly
- **Keep it simple** - Three funds can beat complex portfolios
- **Define your rules** - Contribution, rebalancing, buying, and selling rules
- **Plan for challenges** - Know what you'll do when (not if) markets drop
- **Review regularly** - Annual reviews keep plans relevant
- **Follow the plan** - The best plan works only if you execute it
