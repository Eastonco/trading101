# Chapter 18: Reading Financial Statements

## Overview

Financial statements are the report card of a business. They tell you how much money a company makes, what it owns, what it owes, and where its cash is going. Every serious investor should be able to read and understand the three core financial statements.

---

## The Three Core Statements

### Income Statement (Profit & Loss)

**What it shows:** How much money the company made or lost over a period (quarter or year).

**Key question answered:** Is the company profitable?

### Balance Sheet

**What it shows:** What the company owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and shareholders' stake (equity) at a point in time.

**Key question answered:** Is the company financially healthy?

### Cash Flow Statement

**What it shows:** How cash moved in and out of the company over a period.

**Key question answered:** Is the company generating real cash?

---

## The Income Statement

### Structure

```
Revenue (Sales)
- Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit

- Operating Expenses
  - Selling, General & Administrative (SG&A)
  - Research & Development (R&D)
  - Depreciation & Amortization
= Operating Income (EBIT)

- Interest Expense
= Earnings Before Tax (EBT)

- Income Tax
= Net Income (Earnings)
```

### Key Line Items

**Revenue (Top Line):**
- Total sales before any expenses
- Growth rate matters: 10%+ growth is good for most companies
- Look for consistency vs. one-time spikes

**Gross Profit:**
- Revenue minus direct costs of producing goods/services
- Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue
- Higher margins typically better (40%+ is strong)

**Operating Income (EBIT):**
- Profit from core business operations
- Excludes interest and taxes
- Operating Margin = Operating Income / Revenue
- 15-20%+ is healthy for many industries

**Net Income (Bottom Line):**
- Final profit after all expenses
- What's left for shareholders
- Net Margin = Net Income / Revenue

**Earnings Per Share (EPS):**
- Net Income / Shares Outstanding
- What most investors focus on
- Compare to analyst estimates

### What to Look For

**Positive signs:**
- Growing revenue year-over-year
- Expanding margins over time
- Consistent profitability
- EPS growth

**Warning signs:**
- Revenue declining
- Margins shrinking
- One-time gains masking poor operations
- Inconsistent earnings

---

## The Balance Sheet

### Structure

```
ASSETS = LIABILITIES + SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY

Assets:
- Current Assets (cash, receivables, inventory)
- Long-term Assets (property, equipment, intangibles)

Liabilities:
- Current Liabilities (payables, short-term debt)
- Long-term Liabilities (long-term debt, leases)

Shareholders' Equity:
- Common Stock
- Retained Earnings
```

### Key Concepts

**Current vs. Long-term:**
- Current = converted to cash or due within 1 year
- Long-term = beyond 1 year

**Book Value:**
- Total Equity / Shares Outstanding
- Theoretical liquidation value per share

### Key Line Items

**Cash and Cash Equivalents:**
- Liquid assets
- War chest for opportunities and downturns
- Too much can indicate lack of investment ideas

**Accounts Receivable:**
- Money owed by customers
- Should track with revenue growth
- Rising faster than revenue = collection problems

**Inventory:**
- Products waiting to be sold
- Rising faster than revenue = potential obsolescence
- Just-in-time reduces this

**Property, Plant & Equipment (PP&E):**
- Physical assets
- Depreciation reduces value over time

**Goodwill & Intangibles:**
- Value from acquisitions above book value
- Can be written down (impaired)
- High goodwill = lots of acquisitions at high prices

**Accounts Payable:**
- Money owed to suppliers
- Rising can be good (using supplier financing) or bad (cash problems)

**Debt:**
- Short-term and long-term borrowings
- Debt/Equity ratio matters
- Interest coverage (EBIT/Interest) should be >3x

**Retained Earnings:**
- Accumulated profits not paid as dividends
- Grows if company is profitable

### What to Look For

**Positive signs:**
- Strong cash position
- Low debt relative to equity
- Receivables/inventory tracking with sales
- Growing retained earnings

**Warning signs:**
- Cash declining rapidly
- Debt increasing faster than equity
- Goodwill large relative to total assets
- Negative equity

## The Cash Flow Statement

### Why Cash Flow Matters

**Accounting profit ≠ Cash**

A company can report profits while running out of cash (and vice versa). Cash flow shows the real money.

### Three Sections

**Operating Cash Flow (CFO):**
- Cash from core business operations
- Should be positive and growing
- Most important section

**Investing Cash Flow (CFI):**
- Cash spent on/received from investments
- Usually negative (buying equipment, acquisitions)
- Positive might mean selling assets

**Financing Cash Flow (CFF):**
- Cash from/to investors and creditors
- Debt issuance/repayment
- Stock issuance/buybacks
- Dividends

### Free Cash Flow (FCF)

**FCF = Operating Cash Flow - Capital Expenditures**

- Cash available after maintaining the business
- Can be used for dividends, buybacks, acquisitions, debt payoff
- More reliable than earnings for valuation

### Operating Cash Flow Components

```
Net Income
+ Depreciation/Amortization (non-cash)
+ Decrease in Receivables
- Increase in Receivables
+ Decrease in Inventory
- Increase in Inventory
+ Increase in Payables
- Decrease in Payables
= Cash Flow from Operations
```

### What to Look For

**Positive signs:**
- CFO > Net Income (quality earnings)
- Positive Free Cash Flow
- CFO consistently growing
- Capex reasonable relative to depreciation

**Warning signs:**
- Net Income positive, CFO negative
- Burning cash every quarter
- Free Cash Flow negative for years
- Heavy reliance on financing cash flows

## Reading the Statements Together

### The Cash Flow Cross-Check

**Income Statement says:** $100 million net income
**Cash Flow Statement shows:** Only $50 million CFO

Where's the difference? Usually working capital (receivables, inventory) or non-cash items.

### The Balance Sheet Evolution

Compare balance sheets year-over-year:
- Is cash growing from operations or debt?
- Is debt manageable relative to cash flow?
- Are assets growing productively?

### Example Analysis

**Company XYZ:**
- Revenue: $1B (up 15% YoY)
- Net Income: $100M (10% margin)
- Operating Cash Flow: $130M (greater than NI ✓)
- Free Cash Flow: $80M (after $50M capex)
- Debt: $200M
- Cash: $300M
- Debt/EBITDA: 1.5x

**Assessment:** Healthy. Growing revenue, profitable, generates real cash, modest debt, strong cash position.

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Focusing Only on Revenue or Earnings

All three statements matter. A company can grow revenue while destroying cash.

### 2. Ignoring Cash Flow

Earnings can be manipulated more easily than cash flow. Always check CFO.

### 3. Point-in-Time Balance Sheet

Balance sheets can be window-dressed at quarter end. Look at trends.

### 4. Missing Non-Operating Items

One-time gains/losses can distort earnings. Look at operating income.

### 5. Not Comparing to Peers

$100M in debt means different things for different company sizes.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Income Statement:** Profitability over time - is the company making money?
- **Balance Sheet:** Financial health at a point - what does it own and owe?
- **Cash Flow Statement:** Real cash movement - is profit turning into cash?
- **Cash flow is king** - CFO should exceed net income for quality earnings
- **Free Cash Flow** - What's left after maintaining the business
- **Trends matter** - Compare multiple periods, not just one snapshot
- **Use all three together** - Each tells part of the story
