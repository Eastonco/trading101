# Chapter 19: Valuation Metrics

## Overview

Valuation metrics help you determine whether a stock is cheap, fairly priced, or expensive relative to its fundamentals. While no single metric tells the whole story, understanding these tools helps you make better investment decisions.

---

## Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio

### What It Is

The most widely used valuation metric.

**Formula:** P/E = Stock Price / Earnings Per Share

**Example:**
- Stock price: $50
- EPS: $2.50
- P/E = $50 / $2.50 = 20x

**Interpretation:** Investors are paying $20 for every $1 of earnings.

### Types of P/E

**Trailing P/E:** Uses past 12 months of earnings
**Forward P/E:** Uses estimated future earnings

Forward P/E is more relevant for investing decisions since you're buying future earnings.

### What's a "Good" P/E?

| P/E Range | General Interpretation |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Under 10 | Very cheap (or problems) |
| 10-15 | Value territory |
| 15-25 | Fairly valued |
| 25-40 | Growth premium |
| Over 40 | High growth expectations |

**Important:** P/E varies by:
- Industry (tech > utilities)
- Growth rate (fast growers deserve higher P/E)
- Market conditions (bull markets = higher P/E)
- Interest rates (lower rates = higher P/E)

### P/E Limitations

- Meaningless for unprofitable companies
- Earnings can be manipulated
- Doesn't account for debt
- Doesn't account for growth

---

## PEG Ratio

### What It Is

P/E adjusted for growth rate.

**Formula:** PEG = P/E Ratio / Earnings Growth Rate

**Example:**
- P/E = 25
- Expected earnings growth = 25%
- PEG = 25 / 25 = 1.0

### Interpretation

| PEG | Interpretation |
|-----|----------------|
| Under 1 | Potentially undervalued for its growth |
| 1.0 | Fairly valued for growth |
| Over 2 | Potentially overvalued for growth |

### PEG Advantages

- Allows comparison across different growth rates
- Company with P/E 30 and 30% growth = same PEG as P/E 15 and 15% growth
- Helps identify growth at reasonable price (GARP)

### PEG Limitations

- Relies on growth estimates (which are often wrong)
- Growth rates change over time
- Doesn't work for declining earnings

---

## Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio

### What It Is

Compares price to book value (equity per share).

**Formula:** P/B = Stock Price / Book Value Per Share

Where Book Value Per Share = Total Equity / Shares Outstanding

**Example:**
- Stock price: $60
- Book value: $30
- P/B = 2.0x

### Interpretation

- P/B = 1.0: Trading at book value
- P/B < 1.0: Trading below book value (potential value or value trap)
- P/B > 1.0: Premium to book value (normal for most companies)

### When P/B Is Useful

- **Financial companies:** Banks, insurance (assets are mostly financial)
- **Asset-heavy businesses:** Manufacturing, utilities
- **Distressed situations:** Liquidation analysis

### P/B Limitations

- Book value ≠ market value (assets recorded at historical cost)
- Intangible assets not fully reflected
- Less relevant for service/tech companies
- Negative book value possible

---

## Price-to-Sales (P/S) Ratio

### What It Is

**Formula:** P/S = Stock Price / Revenue Per Share

Or: P/S = Market Cap / Total Revenue

### When to Use P/S

- Unprofitable companies (P/E doesn't work)
- Early-stage growth companies
- Comparing companies with different profitability

### Interpretation

| P/S | General Interpretation |
|-----|------------------------|
| Under 1 | Cheap (or problems) |
| 1-3 | Reasonable |
| 3-10 | Growth premium |
| Over 10 | High expectations |

### P/S Limitations

- Ignores profitability (revenue ≠ profit)
- Low-margin businesses will always have low P/S
- Doesn't account for debt

---

## Enterprise Value Metrics

### What Is Enterprise Value (EV)?

**EV = Market Cap + Debt - Cash**

EV represents the total cost to acquire a company (pay for equity + assume debt - take the cash).

### EV/EBITDA

**Formula:** EV / EBITDA

Where EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, Amortization

### Why EV/EBITDA?

- Accounts for different capital structures (debt levels)
- Allows comparison across companies with different leverage
- Approximates operating cash flow
- Used extensively in M&A

### Interpretation

| EV/EBITDA | General Interpretation |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Under 6 | Cheap |
| 6-10 | Fair value |
| 10-15 | Premium |
| Over 15 | Expensive or high growth |

### EV/Revenue (EV/Sales)

Similar to P/S but accounts for debt. Useful for unprofitable companies.

## Dividend Metrics

### Dividend Yield

**Formula:** Annual Dividend / Stock Price

**Example:**
- Annual dividend: $2.00
- Stock price: $50
- Yield = 4%

### Dividend Payout Ratio

**Formula:** Dividends Per Share / Earnings Per Share

**Interpretation:**
- Under 50%: Conservative, room to grow dividend
- 50-75%: Moderate
- Over 75%: High, may not be sustainable
- Over 100%: Paying out more than earned (unsustainable)

### Dividend Safety

Check:
- Payout ratio from earnings AND cash flow
- Debt levels
- Earnings stability
- Dividend growth history

---

## Free Cash Flow Yield

### What It Is

**Formula:** Free Cash Flow Per Share / Stock Price

Or: Free Cash Flow / Market Cap

### Why It Matters

- Measures cash return on your investment
- More reliable than earnings-based metrics
- Comparable to bond yields

### Interpretation

- FCF Yield of 5-10%: Attractive
- Above 10%: Very attractive (verify it's sustainable)
- Compare to 10-year Treasury yield as baseline

## Putting Metrics Together

### No Single Metric Is Enough

**Example analysis:**

Stock ABC:
- P/E: 15 (cheap?)
- P/B: 0.8 (cheap?)
- EV/EBITDA: 12 (fair)
- FCF Yield: 2% (expensive?)
- Debt/Equity: 300% (high debt)

**Analysis:** The low P/E and P/B might be value traps. High debt is concerning, and low FCF yield suggests the stock isn't as cheap as simple metrics suggest.

### Compare to Peers

Valuation is relative. Compare:
- Same industry
- Similar growth rates
- Similar capital structures

**Example:**
- Company A: P/E 25
- Industry average: P/E 30
- Company A is relatively cheap IF growth is similar

### Compare to History

- Is the stock cheap vs. its own history?
- What P/E has it typically traded at?
- Is there a reason for change?

## Common Valuation Mistakes

### 1. Comparing Across Industries

Tech P/E of 30 ≠ Utility P/E of 30. Context matters.

### 2. Ignoring Growth

P/E of 30 with 30% growth is cheaper than P/E of 15 with 5% growth (by PEG).

### 3. Ignoring Debt

P/E doesn't account for leverage. Use EV-based metrics.

### 4. Valuation = Buy Signal

Cheap stocks can get cheaper. Expensive stocks can get more expensive. Valuation is one factor.

### 5. Using Single Metric

Always use multiple metrics to get a complete picture.

## Quick Valuation Checklist

- [ ] P/E vs. peers and history
- [ ] PEG ratio (P/E relative to growth)
- [ ] EV/EBITDA vs. peers
- [ ] FCF Yield attractive?
- [ ] Debt load reasonable?
- [ ] What's driving the valuation?
- [ ] Is the market missing something?

---

## Key Takeaways

- **P/E is most common** but has limitations (no growth context, no debt)
- **PEG adjusts for growth** - pay up for growth, but reasonably
- **EV/EBITDA accounts for debt** - better for comparisons
- **P/S works for unprofitable companies** - but ignores margins
- **FCF Yield is most reliable** - cash doesn't lie
- **Context matters** - compare to peers, industry, history
- **No single metric** - use multiple perspectives
