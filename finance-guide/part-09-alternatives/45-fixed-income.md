# Chapter 45: Fixed Income Deep Dive

## Overview

Fixed income investments—primarily bonds—provide income, reduce portfolio volatility, and preserve capital. While stocks get more attention, bonds play a crucial role in a diversified portfolio, especially as you approach and enter retirement. This chapter explores the bond universe in depth.

---

## Bond Basics Review

### What Is a Bond?

**A bond is a loan you make to a borrower (government, corporation)** who promises to:
1. Pay you regular interest (coupon)
2. Return your principal at maturity

### Key Bond Terms

**Face value (Par):** The amount returned at maturity (typically $1,000)
**Coupon rate:** Annual interest as percentage of face value
**Coupon payment:** Actual dollar amount paid (usually semi-annually)
**Maturity:** When principal is returned
**Yield:** Current return based on price paid

### Bond Pricing Example

| Feature | Value |
|---------|-------|
| Face value | $1,000 |
| Coupon rate | 5% |
| Coupon payment | $50/year ($25 semi-annually) |
| Current price | $950 |
| Current yield | $50 / $950 = 5.26% |
| Maturity | 10 years |

## Types of Bonds

### Treasury Securities

**Backed by the full faith and credit of the US government.**

| Type | Maturity | Interest |
|------|----------|----------|
| T-Bills | 4-52 weeks | Discount (no coupon) |
| T-Notes | 2-10 years | Semi-annual coupon |
| T-Bonds | 20-30 years | Semi-annual coupon |
| TIPS | Various | Inflation-adjusted |
| I-Bonds | 30 years | Inflation-adjusted |

**Characteristics:**
- Safest bonds (no default risk)
- Exempt from state/local taxes
- Most liquid bond market
- Benchmark for other rates

### Corporate Bonds

**Debt issued by companies to fund operations or growth.**

**Investment grade (rated BBB/Baa or higher):**
- Large, stable companies
- Lower yields than junk
- Lower default risk

**High yield (junk) (rated BB/Ba or lower):**
- Smaller or leveraged companies
- Higher yields (compensating for risk)
- Higher default risk
- More correlated with stocks

### Municipal Bonds (Munis)

**Debt issued by state and local governments.**

**Key feature:** Interest is often exempt from federal taxes (and state taxes if you're a resident).

**Types:**
- General obligation (GO): Backed by taxing power
- Revenue bonds: Backed by specific project revenue (toll road, hospital)

**Tax-equivalent yield:**
Tax-Equivalent Yield = Muni Yield / (1 - Tax Rate)

**Example:**
- Muni yield: 3.5%
- Tax rate: 32%
- Tax-equivalent: 3.5% / 0.68 = 5.15%

**At 32% tax bracket, 3.5% muni equals 5.15% taxable yield.**

### Agency Bonds

**Issued by government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs):**
- Fannie Mae
- Freddie Mac
- Federal Home Loan Banks

**Slightly higher yields than Treasuries**, implicit (not explicit) government backing.

### International Bonds

**Developed market bonds:**
- Similar to US bonds
- Currency risk
- Different interest rate cycles

**Emerging market bonds:**
- Higher yields
- Higher default risk
- Currency volatility

## Interest Rate Risk

### The Inverse Relationship

**When interest rates rise, bond prices fall.**
**When interest rates fall, bond prices rise.**

### Why This Happens

**Example:**
- You own a bond paying 4%
- New bonds issued at 5%
- Your 4% bond is less attractive
- Its price must fall to compete

### Duration

**Duration measures sensitivity to interest rate changes.**

**Approximate rule:** If duration is 7 years, a 1% rate increase causes ~7% price decline.

| Bond Type | Typical Duration | Rate Sensitivity |
|-----------|------------------|------------------|
| T-Bills | 0.25 years | Very low |
| Short-term | 2-3 years | Low |
| Intermediate | 5-7 years | Moderate |
| Long-term | 15-20 years | High |

### Duration Example

| Scenario | Duration | Rate Change | Price Impact |
|----------|----------|-------------|--------------|
| Short bond | 2 years | +1% | -2% |
| Intermediate | 7 years | +1% | -7% |
| Long bond | 20 years | +1% | -20% |

**Longer duration = More interest rate risk.**

## Yield Curve

### What Is the Yield Curve?

**A graph showing yields across different maturities.**

### Normal Yield Curve

**Upward sloping:** Longer maturities have higher yields.

```
Yield
 5% |                    ****
 4% |             *****
 3% |      ******
 2% |******
    |________________________
      3mo  2yr  5yr  10yr 30yr
```

**Normal because:** Investors demand higher yields for locking up money longer.

### Inverted Yield Curve

**Downward sloping:** Short rates higher than long rates.

**Significance:** Often predicts recession (market expects Fed to cut rates).

### Flat Yield Curve

**Similar yields across maturities:** Transition state, uncertainty about direction.

## Bond Investment Strategies

### Buy and Hold

**Strategy:** Purchase bonds and hold to maturity.

**Benefits:**
- Predictable income
- Principal returned at maturity (assuming no default)
- Ignores price fluctuations

**Best for:** Income-focused investors, defined liability matching.

### Bond Ladders

**Strategy:** Buy bonds with staggered maturities.

**Example ladder:**
- $20,000 in 1-year bonds
- $20,000 in 2-year bonds
- $20,000 in 3-year bonds
- $20,000 in 4-year bonds
- $20,000 in 5-year bonds

**When 1-year matures:** Reinvest in new 5-year.

**Benefits:**
- Diversifies interest rate risk
- Regular maturity/reinvestment
- Predictable cash flows

### Barbell Strategy

**Strategy:** Concentrate in short and long maturities, skip the middle.

**Example:**
- 50% in 1-2 year bonds (liquidity, low risk)
- 50% in 20-30 year bonds (higher yield)

**Rationale:** Short bonds for safety, long bonds for yield, nothing in middle.

### Total Return

**Strategy:** Actively trade bonds for capital gains plus income.

**Approach:**
- Anticipate interest rate changes
- Buy before rates fall
- Sell before rates rise
- Requires active management

## Bond Funds vs. Individual Bonds

### Bond Funds (Mutual Funds/ETFs)

**Pros:**
- Diversification (hundreds of bonds)
- Professional management
- Low minimums
- Easy to buy/sell
- Automatic reinvestment

**Cons:**
- No maturity (continuous)
- Interest rate risk always present
- Expense ratios (though low)
- Less control

### Individual Bonds

**Pros:**
- Hold to maturity (get principal back)
- Predictable cash flows
- No ongoing fees
- Control over selection

**Cons:**
- Less diversification
- Higher minimums ($1,000-10,000+ per bond)
- More research required
- Less liquid

### When to Use Each

**Bond funds:** Most investors, especially smaller portfolios
**Individual bonds:** Larger portfolios ($500K+), specific maturity needs, tax optimization

## TIPS: Inflation-Protected Securities

### How TIPS Work

**Principal adjusts with inflation (CPI):**
- If inflation is 3%, principal increases 3%
- Coupon paid on adjusted principal
- At maturity, get higher of original or adjusted principal

### TIPS Example

| Year | CPI | Principal | Coupon (2%) | Payment |
|------|-----|-----------|-------------|---------|
| 0 | - | $1,000 | 2% | $20 |
| 1 | 3% | $1,030 | 2% | $20.60 |
| 2 | 2% | $1,051 | 2% | $21.02 |
| 3 | 4% | $1,093 | 2% | $21.86 |

**At maturity:** Receive $1,093 (inflation-adjusted principal).

### When to Own TIPS

**Consider TIPS when:**
- Concerned about unexpected inflation
- Want purchasing power protection
- Building real (after-inflation) income

## I-Bonds

### What Are I-Bonds?

**Savings bonds with inflation protection** issued by Treasury.

### Key Features

- **Interest:** Fixed rate + inflation rate (CPI)
- **Purchase limit:** $10,000/year electronic + $5,000 paper (tax refund)
- **Tax deferral:** No tax until redemption
- **Minimum hold:** 1 year
- **Penalty:** 3 months interest if redeemed before 5 years

### I-Bond Pros and Cons

**Pros:**
- No interest rate risk (can't lose principal)
- Inflation protection
- Tax deferral
- State/local tax exempt

**Cons:**
- Purchase limits
- Illiquid first year
- Not good for large portfolios

## Bonds in Your Portfolio

### Role of Bonds

**Primary purposes:**
1. Reduce portfolio volatility
2. Provide income
3. Preserve capital
4. Diversify from stocks

### How Much in Bonds?

**Traditional rule:** Age = Bond percentage

**Example:**
- Age 30: 30% bonds, 70% stocks
- Age 60: 60% bonds, 40% stocks

**Modern view:** More flexible based on risk tolerance, goals, income needs.

### Asset Location

**Where to hold bonds:**
- Tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401k): Taxable bonds
- Taxable accounts: Municipal bonds, I-Bonds

## Key Takeaways

- **Bonds provide income, stability, and diversification** - Essential portfolio role
- **Interest rate risk is primary concern** - Longer duration = More sensitivity
- **Credit risk varies by issuer** - Treasuries safest, junk bonds riskiest
- **Municipal bonds offer tax advantages** - Calculate tax-equivalent yield
- **Bond funds suit most investors** - Individual bonds for larger portfolios
- **Duration measures interest rate sensitivity** - Match to your time horizon
- **TIPS and I-Bonds protect against inflation** - Different structures, both useful
- **Bond allocation should increase with age** - As risk tolerance decreases
