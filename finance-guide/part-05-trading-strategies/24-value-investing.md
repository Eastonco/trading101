# Chapter 24: Value Investing

## Overview

Value investing is the discipline of buying stocks for less than they're worth. Popularized by Benjamin Graham and Warren Buffett, this approach focuses on finding companies trading below their intrinsic value and buying with a margin of safety. It requires patience, independent thinking, and the conviction to buy when others are selling.

---

## The Philosophy

### What Is Value Investing?

**Core principle:** A stock is a piece of a business, not a lottery ticket. If you can determine what that business is worth, you can profit when the market prices it incorrectly.

**Key concepts:**
- **Intrinsic value:** The actual worth of a business based on its assets, earnings, and cash flows
- **Margin of safety:** Buying significantly below intrinsic value to protect against errors
- **Mr. Market:** Benjamin Graham's metaphor for the market's emotional swings

### Mr. Market

Imagine you own a business with a partner named Mr. Market. Every day, he offers to buy your share or sell you his at a price he names. Some days he's euphoric and offers high prices. Other days he's depressed and offers low prices.

**The insight:** You don't have to trade with Mr. Market. You can ignore him entirely or only transact when his prices are clearly advantageous.

## Finding Value

### Quantitative Screens

**Valuation metrics to identify candidates:**

| Metric | Value Range | What It Suggests |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| P/E Ratio | Under 15 | Earnings are cheap |
| P/B Ratio | Under 1.5 | Assets are cheap |
| P/FCF | Under 12 | Cash flow is cheap |
| EV/EBITDA | Under 8 | Enterprise is cheap |
| Dividend Yield | Above 3% | Income + potential upside |

### Qualitative Factors

Numbers alone aren't enough. Evaluate:

**Business quality:**
- Durable competitive advantage
- Stable or growing market share
- Consistent profitability through cycles
- Low capital requirements

**Balance sheet strength:**
- Low or manageable debt
- Strong current ratio
- Adequate cash reserves
- No imminent refinancing needs

**Management quality:**
- Track record of capital allocation
- Insider ownership
- Honest communication
- Long-term orientation

## Margin of Safety

### Why It Matters

**You will be wrong.** Even the best analysts make mistakes. The margin of safety protects you when:
- You overestimate earnings power
- You miss hidden liabilities
- The business deteriorates
- The economy weakens

### How Much Margin?

**Benjamin Graham's approach:**
- Buy at 2/3 or less of intrinsic value
- 33%+ margin of safety

**Modern interpretation:**
- 20-30% discount for high-quality businesses
- 40-50% discount for cyclical or uncertain businesses
- Higher quality = lower margin required

### Example Calculation

**Company ABC Analysis:**
- Estimated intrinsic value: $100 per share
- Current market price: $65 per share
- Margin of safety: 35%

**Decision:** With 35% margin of safety and strong fundamentals, this represents a potential value investment.

## Valuation Methods

### Asset-Based Valuation

**Net-Net Working Capital (Graham's favorite):**

Net-Net = Current Assets - Total Liabilities

If stock trades below net-net value, you're buying assets at a discount.

**Example:**
- Current assets: $50 million
- Total liabilities: $20 million
- Net-net value: $30 million
- Market cap: $20 million
- Trading at 67% of liquidation value

### Earnings-Based Valuation

**Normalized earnings approach:**
1. Calculate average earnings over 5-10 years
2. Apply reasonable multiple (10-15x for average business)
3. Compare to current price

**Example:**
- 10-year average EPS: $4.00
- Conservative multiple: 12x
- Estimated value: $48
- Current price: $32
- Margin of safety: 33%

### Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)

**Basic DCF steps:**
1. Project future free cash flows (5-10 years)
2. Estimate terminal value
3. Discount to present value
4. Compare to current price

**Simplified example:**

| Year | Free Cash Flow | Discounted (10%) |
|------|----------------|------------------|
| 1 | $10M | $9.1M |
| 2 | $11M | $9.1M |
| 3 | $12M | $9.0M |
| 4 | $13M | $8.9M |
| 5 | $14M | $8.7M |
| Terminal | $200M | $124M |
| **Total** | | **$168.8M** |

If market cap is $120M, stock trades at 29% discount to DCF value.

## Where to Find Value

### Temporary Problems

Companies facing fixable issues:
- One-time charges affecting earnings
- Temporary demand weakness
- Management transition
- Short-term competitive pressure

**Key question:** Is this problem permanent or temporary?

### Unloved Industries

**Sectors often trading at discounts:**
- Industries facing secular concerns (oil, tobacco)
- Boring businesses (waste management, railroads)
- Complex businesses analysts don't cover
- Small caps below institutional radar

### Spin-Offs

When companies spin off divisions:
- Parent company shareholders often sell
- Spin-off may be too small for institutions
- Creates forced selling regardless of value
- Often undervalued in first 12-18 months

### Post-Crisis Opportunities

After market crashes or sector-specific crises:
- Quality companies get sold indiscriminately
- Prices diverge from fundamentals
- Fear creates opportunity

**2008-2009 examples:**
- Banks trading below book value
- Industrial companies at single-digit P/Es
- Blue chips yielding 5%+

## Value Traps

### What Is a Value Trap?

A stock that looks cheap but deserves to be cheap. It stays cheap or gets cheaper.

### Warning Signs

**Deteriorating business:**
- Declining revenue year after year
- Eroding profit margins
- Losing market share
- No moat against competition

**Balance sheet issues:**
- Rising debt levels
- Declining cash flow
- Working capital problems
- Upcoming maturities without refinancing plan

**Industry decline:**
- Secular headwinds (newspapers, brick-and-mortar retail)
- Technological disruption
- Regulatory threats
- Shrinking addressable market

### How to Avoid Value Traps

1. **Understand why it's cheap** - There's always a reason
2. **Check the trend** - Is the business improving or declining?
3. **Assess the moat** - Can the company protect its profits?
4. **Evaluate management** - Do they have a credible turnaround plan?
5. **Set a thesis** - What has to happen for the stock to rise?

## Building a Value Portfolio

### Diversification

**Graham's approach:**
- Own 20-30 stocks minimum
- No more than 5% in any single position
- Diversify across sectors
- Include some net-nets for protection

**Concentrated value (Buffett's evolution):**
- Own 10-15 high-conviction positions
- Larger positions in best ideas (20-30%)
- Requires deep analysis
- Higher risk, higher potential reward

### Position Sizing

**Based on conviction and margin of safety:**
- Highest conviction + widest margin: 5-10% position
- Medium conviction: 2-5% position
- Speculative value: 1-2% position

### When to Buy More

**Average down only if:**
- Original thesis is intact
- Business fundamentals unchanged
- Margin of safety has increased
- Position size allows for addition

**Never average down into:**
- Deteriorating fundamentals
- Broken thesis
- Already maximum position size

## When to Sell

### Good Reasons to Sell

1. **Reached fair value:** Margin of safety is gone
2. **Thesis was wrong:** You made an analytical error
3. **Fundamentals deteriorated:** Business is worse than expected
4. **Better opportunity:** Higher return elsewhere

### Bad Reasons to Sell

1. **Price dropped:** If thesis is intact, consider buying more
2. **Impatience:** Value takes time to be recognized
3. **Market fear:** Others' emotions aren't investment reasons
4. **Small gain:** Winners can keep winning

### The 2x Rule

Some value investors sell half when a stock doubles:
- Recovers original capital
- Remaining shares are "free"
- Locks in some profit
- Keeps upside exposure

## Patience Required

### Value Takes Time

**Average holding period for value investors:**
- Graham: 2-3 years
- Buffett: "Forever" (though he does sell)
- Realistic expectation: 2-5 years for value to be recognized

### Why Patience Pays

1. **Catalysts take time:** Turnarounds, spin-offs, buyouts
2. **Market recognition is slow:** Wall Street follows momentum
3. **Compounding works:** Quality businesses grow value
4. **Avoiding transactions:** Fewer taxes and fees

### Staying Patient

- Review thesis quarterly, not price daily
- Keep a written investment diary
- Focus on business progress, not stock price
- Remember: you own businesses, not tickers

## Famous Value Investors

### Benjamin Graham

- Father of value investing
- Wrote "Security Analysis" and "The Intelligent Investor"
- Emphasized quantitative factors and margin of safety
- Taught Warren Buffett at Columbia

### Warren Buffett

- Most successful investor in history
- Evolved from Graham's cigar-butt approach
- Focus on wonderful businesses at fair prices
- Concentrated portfolio, long holding periods

### Seth Klarman

- Runs Baupost Group
- Author of "Margin of Safety"
- Emphasis on risk management
- Willingness to hold cash when nothing is cheap

### Howard Marks

- Co-founder of Oaktree Capital
- Famous for his memos on market psychology
- Specialist in distressed debt
- Emphasis on cycle awareness

## Value Investing in Practice

### Sample Analysis: Hypothetical Manufacturing Company

**Company XYZ:**
- Stock price: $35
- EPS (normalized): $5.00
- Book value per share: $40
- Free cash flow per share: $4.50
- Dividend: $1.40 (4% yield)
- Debt/Equity: 0.3x

**Valuation:**
- P/E: 7x (market average: 18x)
- P/B: 0.88x (trading below book)
- P/FCF: 7.8x
- EV/EBITDA: 5x

**Why it's cheap:**
- Cyclical industry currently depressed
- Recent one-time charge hit earnings
- Not covered by many analysts

**Why it might be a value:**
- Industry leader with 30% market share
- Low debt, strong balance sheet
- Management buying back shares
- Insider purchasing

**Estimated intrinsic value:** $55-65
**Margin of safety:** 36-46%

## Key Takeaways

- **Value = buying businesses below intrinsic value** with a margin of safety
- **Mr. Market is emotional** - Take advantage of his mood swings
- **Margin of safety protects against errors** - Always buy with a discount
- **Avoid value traps** - Cheap isn't always good; understand why it's cheap
- **Patience is essential** - Value takes years to be recognized
- **Quality matters** - Wonderful businesses at fair prices beat mediocre businesses at cheap prices
- **Do your own work** - Value investing requires independent thinking
