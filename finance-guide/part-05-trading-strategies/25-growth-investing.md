# Chapter 25: Growth Investing

## Overview

Growth investing focuses on companies expanding revenue and earnings faster than the market average. Rather than buying cheap assets, growth investors pay premium valuations for exceptional businesses with long runways for expansion. The approach requires identifying sustainable growth, understanding competitive dynamics, and managing the volatility that comes with higher expectations.

---

## The Growth Philosophy

### What Is Growth Investing?

**Core principle:** Some companies can compound earnings at 20-30%+ annually for years. Buying these companies—even at premium prices—generates superior returns if growth materializes.

**Key beliefs:**
- Exceptional companies are worth exceptional prices
- Revenue growth drives long-term stock performance
- Market share gains compound over time
- Great businesses get more valuable, not cheaper

### Growth vs. Value

| Factor | Value Investing | Growth Investing |
|--------|-----------------|------------------|
| Primary focus | Low price relative to fundamentals | High growth relative to market |
| Typical P/E | Under 15x | 25x-50x+ |
| Revenue growth | 0-10% | 15-30%+ |
| Valuation approach | Discount to intrinsic value | Premium for quality |
| Time horizon | 2-5 years | 3-10+ years |
| Key risk | Value trap | Multiple compression |

### Why Pay Up?

**The math of compounding:**

Company A (slow growth):
- Year 1 EPS: $2.00, P/E 15 = $30 stock
- 5% growth for 5 years
- Year 5 EPS: $2.55, P/E 15 = $38.25
- Total return: 28%

Company B (fast growth):
- Year 1 EPS: $1.00, P/E 40 = $40 stock
- 25% growth for 5 years
- Year 5 EPS: $3.05, P/E 25 (compressed) = $76.25
- Total return: 91%

Even with multiple compression, the growth company wins.

## Identifying Growth Companies

### Revenue Growth Metrics

**What to look for:**
- Revenue growth > 15% annually (sustained)
- Accelerating growth (even better)
- Large total addressable market (TAM)
- Ability to grow for many years

**Revenue growth tiers:**

| Annual Revenue Growth | Category |
|----------------------|----------|
| 10-15% | Moderate growth |
| 15-25% | Strong growth |
| 25-40% | High growth |
| 40%+ | Hypergrowth |

### Quality of Growth

Not all growth is equal. Evaluate:

**Organic vs. acquisition growth:**
- Organic (internally generated) is more valuable
- Acquisitions can be value-destroying
- Serial acquirers often overpay

**Profitable vs. unprofitable growth:**
- Profitable growth is sustainable
- Unprofitable growth requires funding
- Path to profitability matters

**Recurring vs. one-time growth:**
- Subscription revenue is most valuable
- Repeat customers indicate product quality
- High retention enables compounding

### The Rule of 40

For software and high-growth companies:

**Rule of 40 = Revenue Growth Rate + Profit Margin**

| Score | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| Under 20 | Struggling |
| 20-40 | Acceptable |
| 40+ | Excellent |
| 60+ | Exceptional |

**Examples:**
- 30% growth + 15% margin = 45 (excellent)
- 50% growth + (-10%) margin = 40 (acceptable—growth focused)
- 10% growth + 20% margin = 30 (acceptable—mature business)

## Evaluating Growth Potential

### Total Addressable Market (TAM)

**Key questions:**
- How big is the potential market?
- What's current penetration?
- How much runway remains?
- Is the market growing?

**TAM Example:**

Cloud computing company:
- Global cloud market: $500 billion
- Company revenue: $5 billion
- Current share: 1%
- Potential to 10x before market saturation

### Competitive Position

**Growth requires defending market share:**

**Moat indicators:**
- Network effects (users attract users)
- High switching costs
- Proprietary technology
- Scale advantages
- Brand loyalty

**Warning signs:**
- Commoditizing market
- Well-funded competitors
- Low barriers to entry
- Customer churn

### Unit Economics

**Customer acquisition cost (CAC):**
- How much to acquire a customer?
- Is CAC declining with scale?

**Lifetime value (LTV):**
- How much does a customer generate?
- Is LTV increasing?

**LTV/CAC ratio:**
- Below 3x: Concerning
- 3x-5x: Healthy
- Above 5x: Excellent

### Management Quality

Growth companies need great execution:
- Founder-led often outperforms
- Track record of hitting targets
- Ability to scale operations
- Capital allocation skill
- Vision for the future

## Growth Investing Strategies

### GARP: Growth at a Reasonable Price

**Philosophy:** Buy growth, but don't overpay.

**Metrics:**
- PEG ratio under 1.5
- P/E below growth rate
- Reasonable price-to-sales

**Example:**
- Stock with P/E of 30
- Growing earnings at 25%
- PEG = 30/25 = 1.2 (reasonable)

### Momentum Growth

**Philosophy:** Buy stocks with accelerating growth and rising prices.

**What to look for:**
- Accelerating revenue growth
- Expanding margins
- Beating estimates consistently
- Stock making new highs

**Risk:** Works until it doesn't—momentum can reverse quickly.

### Emerging Growth

**Philosophy:** Find tomorrow's leaders early.

**Characteristics:**
- Small to mid-cap companies
- Newer public companies
- Disruptive business models
- High growth, often unprofitable

**Challenge:** Many will fail—portfolio approach essential.

### Quality Growth

**Philosophy:** Own the best businesses regardless of price.

**Focus on:**
- Dominant market leaders
- Consistent 15%+ growth
- High returns on capital
- Strong balance sheets
- Proven management

**Examples:** Microsoft, Apple, Visa, Costco

## Valuing Growth Stocks

### Traditional Metrics Limitations

**P/E ratio problems:**
- Meaningless for unprofitable companies
- Doesn't capture growth trajectory
- Ignores reinvestment returns

### Better Metrics for Growth

**Price-to-Sales (P/S):**
- Works for unprofitable companies
- Compare to growth rate and margins
- Software: 5-15x P/S common for growth

**EV/Revenue:**
- Accounts for debt and cash
- Better for comparing companies
- Standard metric for high-growth

**Price-to-Growth (PEG):**
- P/E divided by growth rate
- Under 1 suggests undervalued
- Under 2 generally acceptable

### Forward-Looking Valuation

**Reverse DCF approach:**
What growth is implied by current price?

**Example:**
- Stock price: $100
- Current revenue: $10 per share
- Price-to-sales: 10x
- To justify 10x P/S with 20% margins at maturity
- Need revenue to 5x over 10 years
- Implies ~17% annual revenue growth

**Question:** Is 17% growth for 10 years realistic?

## Managing Growth Stock Volatility

### Expect Large Drawdowns

**Growth stock reality:**
- 30-50% drawdowns are normal
- Can happen without fundamental change
- High expectations = high sensitivity to misses

**Historical examples:**
- Amazon fell 95% (2000-2001), then returned 20,000%+
- Netflix fell 80% (2011-2012), then returned 5,000%+
- Even great companies have brutal drawdowns

### Position Sizing

**Given volatility, size appropriately:**
- Limit single positions to 5-10%
- Diversify across 10-20 growth names
- Keep some cash for opportunities
- Never use leverage on growth stocks

### When Drawdowns Are Buying Opportunities

**Buy on dips when:**
- Fundamental thesis intact
- Growth merely slowed, not stopped
- Competitive position unchanged
- Valuation more reasonable

**Don't buy dips when:**
- Growth is decelerating consistently
- Competition winning
- Market opportunity smaller than thought
- Management losing credibility

## Red Flags in Growth Stocks

### Fundamental Warnings

**Decelerating growth:**
- Q1: 40% growth
- Q2: 35% growth
- Q3: 28% growth
- Q4: 20% growth
- Pattern suggests growth maturity

**Margin erosion:**
- Revenue growing but profits shrinking
- May indicate competitive pressure
- Or unsustainable growth spending

**Customer churn:**
- High customer acquisition, low retention
- Revenue growth masking problems
- Sustainable growth requires retention

### Valuation Warnings

**When to be cautious:**
- P/S > 30x (even for growth)
- Stock price far above any reasonable scenario
- "New paradigm" justifications
- Comparisons to Amazon/Google to justify anything

### Management Warnings

- Consistent guide-downs
- Changing growth metrics
- Heavy insider selling
- Focus on stock price over business

## Selling Growth Stocks

### When to Sell

**Thesis broken:**
- Growth decelerating sustainably
- Competitive position weakening
- TAM smaller than expected
- Management execution failing

**Valuation extreme:**
- Price implies unrealistic growth for too long
- Better opportunities elsewhere
- Take some profits, keep exposure

### When Not to Sell

**Temporary setbacks:**
- One weak quarter (if explained)
- Short-term margin pressure for growth
- Market rotation out of growth

**Price increase alone:**
- Great companies can stay expensive
- Selling winners caps upside
- Let compound returns work

### Position Management

**As winners grow:**
- Rebalance to prevent overconcentration
- Trim to reasonable position size (10-15% max)
- But don't eliminate successful positions
- Let core holding continue compounding

## Famous Growth Investors

### Peter Lynch

- Managed Fidelity Magellan Fund
- 29% annual returns over 13 years
- "Invest in what you know"
- Combined growth with value sensibilities

### Philip Fisher

- Author of "Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits"
- Influenced Warren Buffett
- Deep research into growth companies
- Very long holding periods

### T. Rowe Price

- Pioneer of growth investing
- Founded T. Rowe Price Associates
- Focus on companies in "fertile fields"
- Emphasis on management quality

### Cathie Wood

- Founder of ARK Invest
- Focus on disruptive innovation
- High-conviction, concentrated portfolios
- Controversial but influential approach

## Growth Investing in Practice

### Sample Analysis: Cloud Software Company

**Company CloudCo:**
- Revenue: $2 billion
- Revenue growth: 35%
- Gross margin: 75%
- Operating margin: 5%
- Rule of 40: 40 (35 + 5)
- TAM: $100 billion
- Market share: 2%

**Valuation:**
- Market cap: $30 billion
- EV/Revenue: 15x
- P/S: 15x

**Growth thesis:**
- Large TAM with low penetration
- Best-in-class product (high retention)
- Margin expansion as growth moderates
- Path to 20% operating margins at scale

**Risks:**
- Competition from larger tech giants
- Customer budget constraints
- High valuation requires execution
- Macro sensitivity

**Conclusion:** High-quality growth at reasonable (for growth) valuation. Worth owning with appropriate position size.

## Key Takeaways

- **Growth investing buys exceptional businesses** at premium prices
- **Revenue growth is the primary driver** - Faster growth justifies higher multiples
- **Quality of growth matters** - Recurring, profitable, organic growth is best
- **TAM and competitive position determine runway** - How long can growth continue?
- **Expect volatility** - 30-50% drawdowns are normal for growth stocks
- **Don't overpay for mediocre growth** - Use PEG, P/S, Rule of 40 as guides
- **Let winners run** - Selling great companies early caps your upside
- **Position size for volatility** - Diversify and don't use leverage
