# Chapter 7: 401(k) Plans

## Overview

The 401(k) is the primary retirement savings vehicle for most American workers. Named after a section of the Internal Revenue Code, these employer-sponsored plans offer significant tax advantages and often include free money in the form of employer matches.

Understanding how to maximize your 401(k) is one of the most impactful financial decisions you'll make. This chapter covers everything from the basics to advanced strategies.

---

## What Is a 401(k)?

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan that allows you to contribute a portion of your paycheck before taxes (Traditional) or after taxes (Roth). The money grows tax-advantaged until retirement.

### Key Features

- **Payroll deduction:** Contributions automatically withheld from paycheck
- **Tax advantages:** Either tax-deferred (Traditional) or tax-free growth (Roth)
- **Employer match:** Many employers contribute matching funds
- **High contribution limits:** Much higher than IRAs
- **Limited investment options:** You choose from plan-offered funds
- **Portability:** Can roll over to IRA when leaving employer

## Contribution Limits (2024-2025)

| Contribution Type | 2024 | 2025 |
|-------------------|------|------|
| Employee elective deferral | $23,000 | $23,500 |
| Age 50+ catch-up | $7,500 | $7,500 |
| Total employee (50+) | $30,500 | $31,000 |
| Total all sources (under 50) | $69,000 | $70,000 |
| Total all sources (50+) | $76,500 | $77,500 |

**"All sources" includes:** Employee contributions + employer match + employer profit sharing + after-tax contributions

## Traditional vs. Roth 401(k)

Most plans now offer both options. The key difference is when you pay taxes.

### Traditional 401(k)

**Tax treatment:**
- Contributions reduce taxable income now
- Growth is tax-deferred
- Withdrawals taxed as ordinary income in retirement

**Example:**
- You earn $80,000
- Contribute $10,000 to Traditional 401(k)
- Taxable income: $70,000
- At 22% bracket, you save $2,200 in taxes this year

### Roth 401(k)

**Tax treatment:**
- Contributions made with after-tax dollars (no tax break now)
- Growth is tax-free
- Qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free

**Example:**
- You earn $80,000
- Contribute $10,000 to Roth 401(k)
- Taxable income: $80,000 (no deduction)
- In retirement: $10,000 + all growth withdrawn tax-free

### Which Should You Choose?

| Factor | Choose Traditional | Choose Roth |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| Current tax bracket | High | Low |
| Expected retirement bracket | Lower | Higher |
| Years to retirement | Few | Many |
| Tax diversification | Already have Roth | Have all Traditional |
| State taxes | Moving to low-tax state | Moving to high-tax state |

**General guidance:**
- **Young, lower income:** Roth (likely in lowest brackets of career)
- **Peak earning years:** Traditional (highest bracket now)
- **Uncertain:** Split contributions between both

### You Can Do Both

Your $23,000 limit is combined between Traditional and Roth. You can:
- Put $23,000 in Traditional
- Put $23,000 in Roth
- Split: $15,000 Traditional + $8,000 Roth

## The Employer Match

### Free Money

An employer match is the closest thing to free money you'll ever find. Don't leave it on the table.

**Common match formulas:**
- 50% of first 6% contributed (most common)
- 100% of first 3% contributed
- Dollar-for-dollar up to 4%
- 25% of contributions up to 8%

### Example: 50% Match on First 6%

- Your salary: $60,000
- You contribute 6%: $3,600
- Employer matches 50%: $1,800
- Total annual contribution: $5,400
- That's a **50% instant return** on your money

### Vesting Schedules

Employer matches may not be immediately yours. **Vesting** determines when you own the match.

**Common vesting schedules:**

| Type | How It Works |
|------|--------------|
| Immediate | Match is yours right away |
| Cliff vesting | 100% vested after 3 years (0% before) |
| Graded vesting | 20% per year over 5-6 years |

**Example of graded vesting:**
- Year 1: 0% vested
- Year 2: 20% vested
- Year 3: 40% vested
- Year 4: 60% vested
- Year 5: 80% vested
- Year 6: 100% vested

If you leave after Year 3, you keep only 40% of employer contributions.

**Your own contributions are always 100% vested immediately.**

## Investment Options

Unlike brokerage accounts, 401(k) plans offer a limited menu of investments chosen by your employer.

### Typical Options

- **Target-date funds:** All-in-one funds based on retirement year
- **Index funds:** S&P 500, total market, international, bond
- **Actively managed funds:** Stock and bond funds with managers
- **Company stock:** Often available (be cautious)
- **Stable value fund:** Low-risk, bond-like option

### Evaluating Your Options

**What to look for:**
1. **Expense ratios:** Lower is better (under 0.50% for index funds)
2. **Index fund availability:** Prefer passive options
3. **Asset class coverage:** U.S. stocks, international, bonds
4. **Historical performance:** Relative to benchmarks

### Building Your 401(k) Portfolio

**Simple approach:**
- Target-date fund matching your retirement year (one fund, done)

**DIY approach:**
- 60% U.S. stock index
- 25% International stock index
- 15% Bond index

Adjust percentages based on age and risk tolerance.

### Avoid Excessive Company Stock

Many plans offer company stock, sometimes at a discount. Be cautious:

- Your job already depends on the company
- Don't tie your retirement to the same company
- Enron, Lehman employees learned this painfully
- Limit company stock to 10% of portfolio (or less)

## BrokerageLink & Self-Directed Options

### What Is BrokerageLink?

**BrokerageLink (Fidelity), PCRA (Vanguard), Schwab SDBA (Schwab):** A self-directed brokerage window within your 401(k) that allows you to invest in a much wider range of securities beyond your plan's standard menu.

**Standard 401(k) menu:**
- 10-25 mutual funds chosen by employer
- Often limited options
- May have high expense ratios

**With BrokerageLink:**
- Thousands of stocks, ETFs, and mutual funds
- Choose low-cost index funds not in your plan
- Build custom portfolio
- Access to individual stocks (if you want)

### Is BrokerageLink Available in Your Plan?

**Not all 401(k) plans offer this feature.**

**To check:**
1. Log into your 401(k) provider website
2. Look for "BrokerageLink," "PCRA," "SDBA," or "Self-Directed"
3. Contact HR or plan administrator
4. Review your plan documents

**Availability by provider:**
- Fidelity: BrokerageLink
- Vanguard: PCRA (Personal Choice Retirement Account)
- Schwab: Schwab SDBA (Self-Directed Brokerage Account)
- Empower: Self-Directed Brokerage
- Other providers: May or may not offer

### When to Use BrokerageLink

**Good reasons to use:**

**1. Your 401(k) menu is terrible**
- All funds have expense ratios >0.50%
- No good index fund options
- Limited asset class coverage
- Actively managed funds only

**Example:**
- Standard menu: Only high-fee actively managed funds (1.0%+ expense ratios)
- BrokerageLink: Buy VTI (0.03%) and BND (0.03%)
- Savings: ~0.97% annually = $9,700/year on $1M portfolio

**2. You want specific investments not available**
- Individual stocks (company research, concentrated positions)
- Sector-specific ETFs (technology, healthcare)
- International small-cap exposure
- REITs or commodities
- Low-cost alternatives to your plan's options

**3. You want a specific investment strategy**
- Dividend growth portfolio with individual stocks
- Factor-based investing (value, momentum)
- ESG/socially responsible investing
- Halal/Islamic investing options

**4. Asset location optimization**
- Your plan has no good bond options
- Use BrokerageLink for bonds, keep stocks in Roth IRA

### When NOT to Use BrokerageLink

**Stick with standard menu if:**

**1. Your plan menu is already good**
- Low-cost index funds available (≤0.10% expense ratios)
- Adequate asset class coverage
- Target-date fund option
- No compelling reason to complicate

**2. You don't want to manage it**
- BrokerageLink requires active decisions
- No auto-rebalancing
- You're responsible for allocation
- If you prefer "set and forget," stick with standard menu

**3. You're tempted to trade frequently**
- BrokerageLink access can encourage overtrading
- Frequent trading hurts long-term returns
- If you lack discipline, avoid the temptation

**4. Employer match restrictions**
- Some plans restrict match to standard menu only
- Verify match applies to BrokerageLink holdings

### How to Set Up BrokerageLink

**Typical process:**

1. **Enable the option**
   - Log into 401(k) account
   - Find BrokerageLink/SDBA section
   - Agree to terms and conditions
   - May take 1-3 business days to activate

2. **Transfer funds**
   - Choose amount to move (can be partial or full balance)
   - Transfer from standard menu to BrokerageLink
   - Usually takes 1-2 days to settle

3. **Invest the money**
   - Funds arrive as cash in BrokerageLink
   - Must manually invest (won't auto-invest)
   - Place trades for desired investments

4. **Set up future contributions**
   - Decide what % of contributions go to BrokerageLink
   - Often limited to 50-95% of balance
   - Remaining stays in standard menu

### Fees and Restrictions

**Common fees:**
- Annual fee: $0-50 (some plans charge)
- Trading commissions: Usually $0 for stocks/ETFs
- Mutual fund fees: May apply for certain funds
- Check your specific plan

**Common restrictions:**
- Minimum: Often $1,000 minimum to open
- Maximum: Some plans limit to 50-90% of balance
- Investment restrictions:
  - Usually can't buy: Options, futures, margin, penny stocks
  - Can buy: Stocks, ETFs, most mutual funds
- Withdrawal restrictions: Money can't always be immediately withdrawn

### Best Practices

**1. Have a clear strategy**
- Don't open BrokerageLink just to "have access"
- Know exactly what you'll buy and why
- Document your allocation plan

**2. Keep it simple**
- Three-fund portfolio works in BrokerageLink too
- VTI + VXUS + BND = complete portfolio
- Don't overcomplicate just because you can

**3. Mind the employer match**
- Verify match applies to all holdings
- Some plans require X% in standard menu for match
- Don't sacrifice free money for flexibility

**4. Rebalance thoughtfully**
- BrokerageLink requires manual rebalancing
- Set calendar reminders (quarterly or annual)
- Use new contributions to rebalance when possible

**5. Consider when you leave**
- BrokerageLink holdings roll over to IRA like any 401(k)
- May be easier to manage multiple positions in IRA anyway
- Think long-term

### Example: When BrokerageLink Makes Sense

**Scenario:**
- Your 401(k) menu has only actively managed funds
- Average expense ratio: 0.85%
- No S&P 500 index fund
- No international options
- $100,000 balance

**Solution:**
1. Open BrokerageLink
2. Transfer $95,000 (keep $5,000 in standard menu for match)
3. Buy:
   - $57,000 - VTI (0.03% ER)
   - $28,500 - VXUS (0.07% ER)
   - $9,500 - BND (0.03% ER)
4. Set future contributions: 95% to BrokerageLink

**Result:**
- Expense savings: ~0.82% annually
- Better diversification (international exposure)
- $820/year saved on $100,000 (compounds over time)

### Example: When BrokerageLink Doesn't Make Sense

**Scenario:**
- Your 401(k) has Vanguard index funds
- VFIAX (S&P 500): 0.04%
- VTIAX (International): 0.11%
- VBTLX (Bonds): 0.05%
- Target-date funds: 0.08%

**Conclusion:**
- Standard menu is already excellent
- BrokerageLink adds complexity without benefit
- Stick with simple target-date fund or three-fund portfolio in standard menu
- Not worth the extra management

### Special Use Case: Halal/Islamic Investing

**For Muslim investors seeking Shariah-compliant options:**

If your 401(k) standard menu doesn't offer halal funds, BrokerageLink can provide access to:
- HLAL (Wahed Dow Jones Islamic World ETF)
- SPUS (SP Funds S&P 500 Sharia Industry Exclusions ETF)
- UMMA (Wahed FTSE USA Shariah ETF)
- Individual stocks screened for Shariah compliance

See the Ethical & Religious Investing section in Chapter 58 for more details on halal investing principles and implementation.

## Key Features to Understand

### Automatic Enrollment

Many employers automatically enroll employees at 3-6% contribution rate. You can (and should) increase this.

### Automatic Escalation

Some plans automatically increase your contribution rate by 1% annually until reaching a cap. Opt in if available.

### Loans

Most 401(k) plans allow loans against your balance.

**Typical terms:**
- Borrow up to 50% of vested balance (max $50,000)
- Repay over 5 years (or 15 for home purchase)
- Interest paid to yourself

**Why loans are risky:**
- If you leave your job, loan often due immediately
- If you can't repay, treated as distribution (taxes + penalties)
- Borrowed money isn't invested (opportunity cost)
- Creates a habit of raiding retirement funds

**Best practice:** Avoid 401(k) loans. Build an emergency fund instead.

### Hardship Withdrawals

Some plans allow withdrawals for immediate financial hardship:
- Medical expenses
- Home purchase
- Funeral expenses
- Education
- Eviction prevention

**Consequences:**
- Taxes owed on withdrawal
- 10% early withdrawal penalty (if under 59½)
- Permanently reduces retirement savings

Use only as absolute last resort.

## Advanced Strategies

### Mega Backdoor Roth

Some plans allow after-tax contributions above the $23,000 limit, which can be converted to Roth.

**How it works:**
1. Max out your $23,000 pre-tax/Roth contribution
2. Make after-tax contributions (up to $69,000 total limit)
3. Convert after-tax contributions to Roth (in-plan or to Roth IRA)

**Example:**
- $23,000 employee contribution
- $10,000 employer match
- $36,000 after-tax contribution
- = $69,000 total
- Convert $36,000 after-tax to Roth

**Requirements:**
- Plan must allow after-tax contributions
- Plan must allow in-service conversions or withdrawals
- Not all plans offer this

**Check with HR or plan administrator.**

### Strategic Timing of Contributions

If your employer matches per pay period (not true-up):
- Don't front-load contributions too much
- You might max out early and miss matching contributions

**Example:**
- Annual salary: $100,000
- $23,000 contribution limit
- Match: 50% of first 6%

If you contribute 50% of pay ($4,167/month), you max out by June. You'd miss July-December matches.

**Solution:** Spread contributions evenly throughout the year, or verify your plan "trues up" at year-end.

## Leaving Your Job

When you leave an employer, you have options for your 401(k):

### Option 1: Leave It

- Keep money in former employer's plan
- No tax consequences
- May have limited fund options or higher fees
- Can be forgotten about

### Option 2: Roll to New Employer's 401(k)

- Transfer to new job's plan
- No tax consequences if done correctly
- Consolidates accounts
- Subject to new plan's options

### Option 3: Roll to IRA (Most Common)

- Transfer to Traditional or Roth IRA
- No tax consequences if done correctly
- More investment options
- Potentially lower fees
- More control

### Option 4: Cash Out (Don't Do This)

- Receive money as cash
- Mandatory 20% withholding
- Owe income taxes on full amount
- 10% early withdrawal penalty if under 59½

**Example of cashing out $50,000 at age 35:**
- 20% withheld: $10,000
- Additional taxes (22% bracket): ~$11,000 (depending on actual tax situation)
- 10% penalty: $5,000
- Net received: ~$24,000-30,000
- Lost: 40-50% plus all future growth

Never cash out. Always roll over.

### How to Roll Over

**Direct rollover (best):**
- Money goes directly from old plan to new plan/IRA
- Never touches your hands
- No withholding or tax issues

**Indirect rollover:**
- Check sent to you
- 20% withheld
- Must deposit full amount (including withheld portion) within 60 days
- Get withholding back when you file taxes

Always request a **direct rollover** to avoid complications.

## 401(k) Withdrawal Rules

### Age 59½

- Penalty-free withdrawals allowed
- Still owe income tax on Traditional

### Age 55

- If you separate from employer at 55+, penalty-free access to that employer's 401(k)
- Called "Rule of 55"
- Doesn't apply to IRAs

### Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

- Traditional 401(k): Must start at age 73 (SECURE 2.0)
- Roth 401(k): Now exempt from RMDs starting 2024
- If still working at 73, can delay RMDs from current employer's plan

### Early Withdrawal

Before 59½:
- 10% penalty + income taxes
- Some exceptions (disability, death, QDRO, etc.)

## 401(k) vs. IRA

| Feature | 401(k) | IRA |
|---------|--------|-----|
| 2024 limit | $23,000 | $7,000 |
| Employer match | Yes | No |
| Investment options | Limited | Unlimited |
| Loan option | Usually | No |
| Age 55 rule | Yes | No |
| Creditor protection | Strong | Varies by state |

**Optimal strategy:** Contribute enough to 401(k) to get full match, then consider maxing IRA, then max 401(k).

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Not Contributing Enough to Get Full Match

Leaving free money on the table. At minimum, contribute enough to maximize employer match.

### 2. Cashing Out When Changing Jobs

Devastating long-term impact. Always roll over.

### 3. Being Too Conservative

Young investors with decades until retirement shouldn't be 50% in bonds. Time allows for equity-heavy allocation.

### 4. Ignoring Fees

A 1% expense ratio vs. 0.05% costs tens of thousands over a career. Know what you're paying.

### 5. Not Increasing Contributions Over Time

Start at 6%, increase by 1% with each raise until you're maxing out.

### 6. Over-Investing in Company Stock

Concentration risk + employment risk = dangerous combination.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Get the full employer match** - It's free money, 50-100% instant return
- **Understand Traditional vs. Roth** - Tax now (Roth) or later (Traditional)
- **Watch your vesting schedule** - Employer match may not be immediately yours
- **Keep fees low** - Choose index funds when available
- **Never cash out** - Always roll over when changing jobs
- **Increase contributions over time** - Work toward maxing out the $23,000 limit
- **Don't over-concentrate in company stock** - Diversification matters
