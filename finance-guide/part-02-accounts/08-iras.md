# Chapter 8: Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs)

## Overview

Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) are personal retirement savings accounts with significant tax advantages. Unlike 401(k)s, IRAs aren't tied to your employer—you open and manage them yourself, giving you complete control over investment choices.

This chapter covers Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, and the backdoor strategies that make IRAs accessible even to high earners.

---

## Types of IRAs

### Traditional IRA

**Tax treatment:**
- Contributions may be tax-deductible
- Growth is tax-deferred
- Withdrawals taxed as ordinary income

### Roth IRA

**Tax treatment:**
- Contributions are not tax-deductible
- Growth is tax-free
- Qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free

### SEP IRA (Simplified Employee Pension)

- For self-employed and small business owners
- High contribution limits (up to 25% of compensation, max $69,000 in 2024)
- Only employer contributions (no employee elective)

### SIMPLE IRA

- For small businesses (under 100 employees)
- Lower limits than 401(k) ($16,000 employee + employer match)
- Less administrative burden than 401(k)

This chapter focuses on Traditional and Roth IRAs, the most common types for individual investors.

---

## Contribution Limits (2024-2025)

| Type | 2024 | 2025 |
|------|------|------|
| IRA contribution limit | $7,000 | $7,500 |
| Age 50+ catch-up | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Total (50+) | $8,000 | $8,500 |

**Important:** The limit is combined across all your Traditional and Roth IRAs. You can't contribute $7,000 to each.

### Contribution Deadline

You have until the tax filing deadline (typically April 15) to make IRA contributions for the previous year. This gives you extra time to fund your accounts.

## Traditional IRA

### Who Can Contribute?

Anyone with earned income can contribute to a Traditional IRA. However, the tax deduction may be limited.

### Deductibility Rules

**No retirement plan at work:**
Full deduction regardless of income

**Have retirement plan at work:**

| Filing Status | 2024 Phase-Out Range | Full Deduction |
|--------------|---------------------|----------------|
| Single | $77,000 - $87,000 | Under $77,000 |
| Married (both have plans) | $123,000 - $143,000 | Under $123,000 |
| Married (only spouse has plan) | $230,000 - $240,000 | Under $230,000 |

**Above phase-out:** No deduction, but you can still make non-deductible contributions

### Non-Deductible Traditional IRA Contributions

If you can't deduct, should you contribute anyway?

**Pros:**
- Tax-deferred growth
- Sets up backdoor Roth strategy

**Cons:**
- Complicated tax tracking (Form 8606)
- Earnings taxed at ordinary rates (not capital gains)
- Must track basis forever

**Generally:** If you can't deduct, use Roth IRA directly or via backdoor.

### Withdrawal Rules

- **Before 59½:** 10% penalty + income taxes
- **After 59½:** Income taxes only
- **RMDs:** Must begin at age 73

## Roth IRA

### The Power of Tax-Free Growth

A Roth IRA is one of the most powerful investment accounts available. Money goes in after-tax, but all growth and qualified withdrawals are completely tax-free—forever.

### Who Can Contribute?

Income limits apply to direct Roth IRA contributions.

| Filing Status | 2024 Phase-Out | Full Contribution |
|--------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Single | $146,000 - $161,000 | Under $146,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $230,000 - $240,000 | Under $230,000 |

**Above phase-out:** Can't contribute directly, but backdoor Roth works

### Contribution Rules

- Must have earned income
- Contribution can't exceed earned income
- Can contribute even if covered by workplace plan
- Can contribute at any age (no age limit)

### Qualified Withdrawals

To be tax-free and penalty-free, withdrawals must be "qualified":

1. **5-year rule:** Account open for at least 5 years
2. **Age 59½** OR qualifying event (death, disability, first home purchase)

### Flexibility: Withdraw Contributions Anytime

Unlike Traditional IRAs, you can withdraw your Roth **contributions** (not earnings) at any time, for any reason, tax and penalty-free.

**Example:**
- You've contributed $50,000 over the years
- Account is now worth $80,000
- You can withdraw up to $50,000 anytime with no penalty
- The $30,000 in earnings is subject to rules

This makes Roth IRAs a powerful emergency backup (though you shouldn't treat them as a piggy bank).

### No RMDs

Roth IRAs have no Required Minimum Distributions during your lifetime. The money can grow tax-free as long as you live, making them excellent for estate planning.

### First-Time Home Purchase

Up to $10,000 in earnings can be withdrawn penalty-free for a first-time home purchase (5-year rule must still be met). This is a lifetime limit.

## Traditional vs. Roth IRA Decision

### Choose Traditional If:
- You're in a high tax bracket now
- You expect lower income in retirement
- You need the tax deduction now
- You're in peak earning years

### Choose Roth If:
- You're in a low tax bracket now
- You expect higher income in retirement
- You want tax-free income in retirement
- You value flexibility (contribution withdrawals)
- You want to avoid RMDs

### The Math Example

**Scenario:** $7,000 contribution, 25 years, 8% return, 22% tax bracket now

**Traditional IRA:**
- Invest $7,000 (saving $1,540 in taxes now)
- Grows to $47,926
- Withdraw at 22% bracket: Net $37,382

**Roth IRA:**
- Pay $1,540 in taxes to invest $7,000 (or invest $7,000 and pay from other funds)
- Grows to $47,926
- Withdraw tax-free: $47,926

**If tax brackets are equal:** Roth wins slightly due to withdrawal flexibility
**If future bracket is lower:** Traditional wins
**If future bracket is higher:** Roth wins

## Backdoor Roth IRA

### The Problem

High earners ($161,000+ single, $240,000+ married in 2024) can't contribute directly to a Roth IRA.

### The Solution

The "backdoor" Roth—completely legal and IRS-approved:

1. Contribute to a Traditional IRA (non-deductible)
2. Convert the Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA
3. Pay taxes only on any growth between steps 1 and 2 (usually minimal)

### Step-by-Step

1. **Open Traditional IRA** (if not already open)
2. **Contribute $7,000** (non-deductible)
3. **Wait briefly** (some suggest a few days, others convert immediately)
4. **Convert to Roth IRA** (online or via form)
5. **File Form 8606** with tax return to document non-deductible contribution
6. **Pay tax on any growth** during the conversion (usually trivial)

### The Pro-Rata Rule (Important!)

If you have existing pre-tax money in any Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRA, the conversion is taxed proportionally.

**Example:**
- Existing Traditional IRA: $93,000 (pre-tax)
- New non-deductible contribution: $7,000
- Total: $100,000 (93% pre-tax, 7% after-tax)
- Convert $7,000: 93% ($6,510) is taxable

**Solution:** Roll existing Traditional IRA into your 401(k) before doing backdoor Roth. This removes pre-tax IRA money from the calculation.

### Is Backdoor Roth Legal?

Yes. Congress has known about this strategy for years and has not closed it. The Build Back Better bill attempted to end it but was not passed. As of 2024, backdoor Roth remains legal.

## Roth Conversions

Beyond the backdoor strategy, you can convert existing Traditional IRA money to Roth at any time.

### Why Convert?

- Lock in current tax rates
- Move money to tax-free growth
- Reduce future RMDs
- Estate planning benefits

### Conversion Tax Consequences

The converted amount is added to your taxable income for the year. This can be substantial.

**Example:**
- Convert $50,000 from Traditional to Roth
- This $50,000 is taxable income
- At 24% bracket: $12,000 tax bill

### Strategic Conversion

**Best times to convert:**
- Low-income years (job change, sabbatical)
- Early retirement before Social Security/pension
- Market downturns (convert same shares at lower dollar value)
- Years with large deductions

### Tax Bracket Filling

Convert just enough each year to "fill up" your current bracket without jumping to the next one.

**Example:**
- Single filer, 24% bracket ends at $191,950
- Current income: $150,000
- Can convert $41,950 while staying in 24% bracket

## IRA Investment Strategies

### Asset Location

Since IRA withdrawals (Traditional) are taxed as ordinary income, consider:

**Good in Traditional IRA:**
- Bonds (interest taxed at ordinary rates anyway)
- REITs (dividends taxed as ordinary income)
- High-turnover funds

**Good in Roth IRA:**
- Highest expected growth assets
- Stocks with appreciation potential
- Assets you want to never pay taxes on

**Good in Taxable:**
- Index stock ETFs (tax-efficient)
- Municipal bonds (tax-free interest)

### Keeping It Simple

A target-date fund in your IRA is perfectly fine. Don't overcomplicate.

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Excess Contributions

Contributing more than the limit results in a 6% penalty per year until corrected.

**Solution:** Withdraw excess before tax deadline, or recharacterize.

### 2. Not Filing Form 8606

If you make non-deductible Traditional IRA contributions or do a backdoor Roth, you must file Form 8606. Failure to file can result in penalties and complicated tax situations.

### 3. Ignoring the Pro-Rata Rule

Many people attempt backdoor Roth without understanding the pro-rata implications, creating unexpected tax bills.

### 4. Converting Too Much at Once

Large conversions can push you into higher tax brackets or trigger Medicare premium surcharges (IRMAA).

### 5. Forgetting the 5-Year Rule

Each Roth conversion has its own 5-year waiting period for penalty-free withdrawal of the converted amount (if under 59½).

## IRA vs. 401(k) Priority

**Optimal contribution order:**

1. 401(k) up to employer match (free money)
2. Max out Roth IRA ($7,000)
3. Max out 401(k) ($23,000)
4. Backdoor Roth if high earner
5. Mega backdoor Roth if available
6. Taxable brokerage

*Note: Individual circumstances vary. Some prefer 401(k) over IRA for creditor protection or specific fund options.*

## Key Takeaways

- **Traditional IRA:** Tax deduction now, taxed later; deduction may be limited by income
- **Roth IRA:** No deduction, but tax-free growth and withdrawals forever
- **Income limits exist for Roth** - But backdoor Roth works for high earners
- **Roth is incredibly flexible** - Contributions withdrawable anytime
- **Backdoor Roth requires attention** - Pro-rata rule can create tax issues
- **Conversions are strategic** - Time them for low-income years
- **No RMDs for Roth** - Money can grow tax-free for life
