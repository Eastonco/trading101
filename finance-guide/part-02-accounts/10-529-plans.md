# Chapter 10: 529 Education Savings Plans

## Overview

529 plans are tax-advantaged accounts designed for education expenses. Named after Section 529 of the Internal Revenue Code, these plans offer tax-free growth and withdrawals when used for qualified education costs—from kindergarten through graduate school.

The SECURE 2.0 Act added a game-changing feature: unused 529 funds can now be rolled into a Roth IRA, eliminating the fear of over-saving.

---

## How 529 Plans Work

### Basic Structure

- Open an account with a 529 plan (typically state-sponsored)
- Contribute after-tax dollars
- Money grows tax-free
- Withdrawals for qualified education expenses are tax-free

### Tax Benefits

**Federal:**
- No contribution deduction (after-tax money)
- Tax-free growth
- Tax-free qualified withdrawals

**State:**
- Many states offer tax deductions/credits for contributions
- Some states offer deductions for any state's plan
- Some only for their own state's plan

---

## Types of 529 Plans

### Education Savings Plans

The most common type. Works like an investment account:

- You choose investments from plan's menu
- Account value fluctuates with market
- Use funds at any eligible institution
- Flexible timing

### Prepaid Tuition Plans

Less common. Locks in current tuition rates:

- Buy credits at today's prices
- Credits used at participating schools (usually in-state public)
- Protects against tuition inflation
- Limited flexibility

**Most investors choose Education Savings Plans** for flexibility.

---

## Contribution Limits

### Annual Limits

There's no explicit annual limit, but contributions are considered gifts for tax purposes.

**Gift tax exclusion (2024):** $18,000 per donor, per beneficiary ($36,000 for married couples)

**5-year gift averaging:** You can contribute up to $90,000 ($180,000 married) at once, treated as if spread over 5 years for gift tax purposes.

### Lifetime Limits

Each state sets its own aggregate limit (typically $235,000-$550,000 per beneficiary). These are soft limits—you can't contribute more, but the account can grow beyond.

## State Tax Deductions

### How It Works

Many states offer tax deductions or credits for 529 contributions.

**Examples:**

| State | Deduction (Single) | Deduction (Married) | Notes |
|-------|-------------------|--------------------| ------|
| New York | $5,000 | $10,000 | Own plan only |
| Virginia | $4,000 | $4,000 per account | Own plan only |
| Pennsylvania | $18,000 | $36,000 | Any state's plan |
| Colorado | Unlimited | Unlimited | Own plan only |
| California | None | None | - |

### Which State's Plan?

**If your state offers deduction only for its plan:**
- Compare deduction value vs. plan quality
- A good plan with no deduction might beat a poor plan with deduction

**If your state offers deduction for any plan (or no deduction):**
- Choose the best plan regardless of state

### Best 529 Plans (Consistently Rated)

- Utah (my529)
- Nevada (Vanguard)
- New York (Direct Plan)
- Ohio (CollegeAdvantage)
- California (ScholarShare)

Look for low fees, good investment options, and no hidden charges.

## Qualified Education Expenses

### Higher Education (College, Graduate School)

- Tuition and fees
- Books and supplies
- Room and board (at least half-time enrollment)
- Computer equipment
- Special needs services

### K-12 Education

- Tuition only (up to $10,000/year per beneficiary)
- Does NOT include books, supplies, room

### Apprenticeships

Registered apprenticeship programs qualify (fees, books, supplies, equipment).

### Student Loan Repayment

Up to $10,000 lifetime per beneficiary can pay student loans.

---

## Non-Qualified Withdrawals

Using 529 money for non-qualified expenses results in:

**On the earnings portion:**
- Income tax at your rate
- 10% penalty

**Contributions are never taxed or penalized** (they were after-tax money).

**Example:**
- Account balance: $50,000
- Contributions: $35,000
- Earnings: $15,000
- Non-qualified withdrawal: $10,000

Proportionally: $7,000 contributions (tax-free) + $3,000 earnings (taxed + 10% penalty)

## The SECURE 2.0 Roth IRA Rollover

### Game-Changing Feature

Starting in 2024, unused 529 funds can be rolled over to a Roth IRA for the beneficiary.

### Requirements

1. **529 account must be open 15+ years**
2. **Contributions from last 5 years ineligible** for rollover
3. **Annual rollover limited to Roth IRA contribution limit** ($7,000 in 2024)
4. **Lifetime rollover limit: $35,000**
5. **Beneficiary must have earned income** equal to rollover amount

### Why This Matters

The biggest fear with 529s was over-saving. What if your child:
- Gets a scholarship?
- Doesn't go to college?
- Goes to a cheaper school?

Now you can convert up to $35,000 to a Roth IRA, giving the beneficiary a massive head start on retirement savings.

### Example Strategy

- Child born, open 529 immediately
- Contribute modestly over 18 years
- Child gets partial scholarship, doesn't use all funds
- At age 23+, roll $7,000/year to Roth IRA
- By age 28, $35,000 in Roth IRA (tax-free forever)

## 529 Strategies

### Start Early

Compound growth matters. A 529 opened at birth has 18 years to grow before college.

**Example:**
- $200/month for 18 years at 7% = ~$86,000
- Total contributed: $43,200
- Growth: ~$43,000

### Superfunding

Use 5-year gift averaging to front-load contributions:

- Grandparents contribute $90,000 at birth (per person)
- 18 years at 7% = ~$290,000

This maximizes tax-free growth time.

### Don't Ignore Your State Deduction

If your state offers 5% tax rate and $10,000 deduction limit:
- $10,000 contribution
- $500 immediate tax savings
- That's a 5% instant return before investment growth

### Multiple Beneficiaries

- Each child can be beneficiary of their own 529
- Unused funds can transfer to siblings
- Consider future grandchildren too

## 529 vs. Other Education Savings Options

### Coverdell Education Savings Account (ESA)

| Feature | 529 Plan | Coverdell ESA |
|---------|----------|---------------|
| Contribution limit | High (~$235-550K total) | $2,000/year |
| Income limits | None | $110K single, $220K married |
| K-12 expenses | Tuition only | All expenses |
| Investment options | Plan's menu | Full brokerage |
| Age limit for use | None | Must use by 30 |

**529 usually wins** due to higher limits and no income restrictions.

### UTMA/UGMA (Custodial Accounts)

| Feature | 529 Plan | UTMA/UGMA |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| Tax treatment | Tax-free for education | Taxable (kiddie tax) |
| Use restrictions | Education only | Any purpose |
| Financial aid impact | Favorable (parental asset) | Unfavorable (student asset) |
| Control | You control | Child controls at majority |

**529 usually wins** for education savings due to tax benefits.

## Financial Aid Considerations

### 529 Owned by Parent

Treated as parental asset:
- Only 5.64% of asset counted in Expected Family Contribution (EFC)
- Withdrawals not counted as income

### 529 Owned by Grandparent

Historically problematic, but FAFSA changes improving:
- Starting 2024-2025 FAFSA, grandparent-owned 529 distributions no longer counted as student income

### Scholarships and 529

If student receives scholarship:
- Can withdraw 529 funds equal to scholarship amount
- No 10% penalty (but earnings still taxed)
- Better: Use 529 for remaining qualified expenses

## Common Mistakes

### 1. Choosing a Plan Based on State Name Alone

Your state's plan may not be the best. Compare fees and options.

### 2. Not Starting Early Enough

Time is your biggest advantage. Even small contributions matter if started early.

### 3. Being Too Conservative

With 15-20 year time horizon, aggressive allocation makes sense early on.

### 4. Forgetting State Tax Deduction

Free money if your state offers it. Max out before taxable investments.

### 5. Assuming Over-Saving Is Catastrophic

With Roth IRA rollover option, this risk is significantly reduced.

### 6. Not Considering All Beneficiaries

Funds can transfer between family members. Don't feel locked into one child.

## 529 Investment Strategy

### Age-Based Portfolios

Most plans offer age-based options that automatically become more conservative as the beneficiary approaches college:

- Age 0-6: 90% stocks
- Age 7-10: 80% stocks
- Age 11-13: 70% stocks
- Age 14-16: 60% stocks
- Age 17-18: 50% stocks

### Static Portfolios

You choose the allocation and it doesn't change automatically.

**Good for:** Those who want control and will rebalance manually.

### Recommended Approach

For most people, age-based portfolios are appropriate. They require no maintenance and automatically de-risk as college approaches.

## Opening a 529 Account

### Direct vs. Advisor-Sold Plans

**Direct-sold plans:**
- Open yourself online
- Lower fees
- No commission

**Advisor-sold plans:**
- Purchased through financial advisor
- Higher fees (load charges)
- May offer same underlying investments

**Almost always choose direct-sold plans.**

### Steps to Open

1. Choose a plan (your state's or best available)
2. Go to plan's website
3. Provide beneficiary information (can be yourself)
4. Link bank account
5. Set up contributions
6. Choose investments

## Key Takeaways

- **Tax-free growth and withdrawals** for qualified education expenses
- **State tax deductions** can add immediate returns
- **High contribution limits** allow substantial education funding
- **SECURE 2.0 Roth rollover** eliminates over-saving fear ($35,000 lifetime limit)
- **Start early** to maximize compound growth
- **Choose the right plan** - fees and investment options matter more than state name
- **Flexible beneficiary** - Can change to siblings, other relatives, or yourself
