# Chapter 6: Brokerage Accounts

## Overview

A brokerage account is your gateway to investing. It's where you buy and sell stocks, bonds, ETFs, and other securities. Unlike retirement accounts, brokerage accounts have no contribution limits, withdrawal restrictions, or special tax advantages—but they offer complete flexibility.

This chapter covers how brokerage accounts work, the different types available, how to choose a broker, and the tax implications you need to understand.

---

## What Is a Brokerage Account?

A brokerage account is a taxable investment account held at a brokerage firm. You deposit money, use it to buy investments, and can withdraw funds at any time.

### Key Characteristics

- **No contribution limits:** Invest as much as you want
- **No withdrawal restrictions:** Access your money anytime
- **Taxable:** Pay taxes on dividends, interest, and capital gains
- **Flexible:** Buy any security the broker offers
- **No age restrictions:** Open at any age (custodial accounts for minors)

### Comparison to Retirement Accounts

| Feature | Brokerage Account | 401(k)/IRA |
|---------|-------------------|------------|
| Contribution limit | None | $23,000/$7,000 (2024) |
| Withdrawal restrictions | None | Penalties before 59½ |
| Tax advantages | None | Tax-deferred or tax-free |
| Investment options | Almost anything | Plan-dependent |
| Required distributions | None | Yes (Traditional) |

## Types of Brokerage Accounts

### Individual Account

The most common type. One person owns the account.

- Sole owner makes all decisions
- Only your name on the account
- Passes through your estate upon death

### Joint Account

Owned by two or more people.

**Types of joint ownership:**

**Joint Tenants with Rights of Survivorship (JTWROS)**
- Most common for married couples
- Upon one owner's death, ownership automatically transfers to survivor
- Avoids probate

**Tenants in Common (TIC)**
- Each owner has a specific percentage
- Upon death, share goes to owner's estate (not other owners)
- Used when owners want different beneficiaries

**Community Property (some states)**
- Available in community property states
- Each spouse owns 50%
- Special tax treatment

### Custodial Accounts (UGMA/UTMA)

Accounts for minors, managed by an adult custodian until the child reaches majority (18-21 depending on state).

**Key points:**
- Assets belong to the child (irrevocable gift)
- "Kiddie tax" rules apply
- Child gains full control at majority
- Can impact college financial aid

### Trust Accounts

Accounts held within a trust structure.

- Revocable trusts: Estate planning tool
- Irrevocable trusts: Asset protection, tax planning
- Requires trust documentation

## Cash vs. Margin Accounts

### Cash Account

- Pay full price for securities
- Cannot borrow against holdings
- No risk of margin call
- Settlement rules apply (T+1)

**Best for:** Most investors, beginners, those wanting to avoid leverage risk

### Margin Account

- Can borrow money from broker to buy securities
- Securities serve as collateral
- Pay interest on borrowed funds
- Can short sell stocks

**Margin Requirements:**
- Initial margin: 50% (must put up half the purchase price)
- Maintenance margin: 25% (minimum equity required)
- Margin call: If equity falls below maintenance, must deposit more

**Example:**
- You have $10,000 in a margin account
- With 50% initial margin, you can buy $20,000 of stock
- If stock drops 30% to $14,000, your equity is $4,000
- Equity percentage: $4,000 / $14,000 = 28.5%
- If maintenance is 25%, you're okay (barely)
- If it drops further, you'll receive a margin call

**Risks of Margin:**
- Losses are magnified
- Interest costs reduce returns
- Margin calls can force sales at worst times
- Can lose more than your investment

**Best for:** Experienced traders who understand the risks

### Payment for Order Flow (PFOF)

Many "free" brokers make money by routing your orders to market makers who pay for that order flow.

**What it means:**
- You may not get the absolute best price
- Price improvement is possible but not guaranteed
- Controversial practice
- Robinhood and others rely heavily on PFOF

**Fidelity and Vanguard do not accept PFOF for equity orders.**

## Tax Implications

This is where brokerage accounts differ most from retirement accounts: **every transaction can trigger taxes.**

### Dividends

**Qualified Dividends:**
- Held for 60+ days around ex-dividend date
- Taxed at preferential rates: 0%, 15%, or 20%
- Most U.S. company dividends qualify

**Ordinary (Non-Qualified) Dividends:**
- Taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%)
- Includes REITs, money markets, short holding periods

### Capital Gains

**Short-Term Capital Gains:**
- Assets held less than one year
- Taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%)

**Long-Term Capital Gains:**
- Assets held more than one year
- Preferential rates: 0%, 15%, or 20%

| Taxable Income (Single) | Long-Term Rate |
|-------------------------|----------------|
| Under $44,625 | 0% |
| $44,625 - $492,300 | 15% |
| Over $492,300 | 20% |

*2024 thresholds; adjusted annually for inflation*

**Net Investment Income Tax:** Additional 3.8% on investment income for high earners (over $200,000 single / $250,000 married).

### Tax-Loss Harvesting

Strategy to reduce taxes by selling losing investments to offset gains.

**How it works:**
1. Sell investment that's down (realize the loss)
2. Loss offsets capital gains
3. If losses exceed gains, deduct up to $3,000 from ordinary income
4. Excess losses carry forward

**Wash Sale Rule:**
- Cannot repurchase "substantially identical" security within 30 days
- Applies 30 days before AND after the sale
- If violated, loss is disallowed (but added to cost basis)

**Example:**
- You have $10,000 in capital gains
- You harvest $8,000 in losses
- Net taxable gain: $2,000
- At 15% rate, you saved $1,200 in taxes

### Cost Basis Methods

When you sell, you need to determine which shares you're selling.

**FIFO (First In, First Out):** Oldest shares sold first (default)
**LIFO (Last In, First Out):** Newest shares sold first
**Specific Identification:** You choose which shares to sell
**Average Cost:** Available for mutual funds only

**Specific identification** is usually optimal for tax management—sell highest-cost shares first to minimize gains.

### Wash Sale Example

You bought VTI shares:
- January: 10 shares at $200
- March: 10 shares at $220
- November: VTI is at $190

If you sell March shares at a $300 loss ($220 - $190 × 10):
- Within 30 days, you buy more VTI
- Wash sale rule triggered
- Loss is disallowed
- Cost basis of new shares increases by $300

**Workaround:** Buy a similar but not "substantially identical" fund (e.g., ITOT instead of VTI).

## Tax-Efficient Investing in Taxable Accounts

### Asset Location Strategy

Place tax-inefficient investments in tax-advantaged accounts:

**In Taxable Accounts (tax-efficient):**
- Index ETFs (low turnover)
- Tax-managed funds
- Individual stocks (control timing)
- Municipal bonds

**In Tax-Advantaged Accounts (tax-inefficient):**
- REITs (high dividends taxed as ordinary income)
- High-yield bonds
- Actively managed funds (high turnover)
- Taxable bond funds

### Index Funds Are Tax-Efficient

Why index funds work well in taxable accounts:
- Low turnover (few capital gains distributions)
- ETF creation/redemption mechanism avoids gains
- You control when to realize gains by selling

### Avoid These in Taxable Accounts

- **Actively managed funds:** Frequent trading creates taxable distributions
- **REITs:** Dividends taxed as ordinary income
- **Short-term trading:** Gains taxed at higher ordinary rates
- **Balanced funds:** Less control over asset location

## Account Transfers

### ACATS Transfer

Moving your account from one broker to another:

1. Open account at new broker
2. Initiate transfer request (at new broker)
3. Old broker sends assets (3-6 business days)
4. Assets arrive "in kind" (no selling required)

**Important:**
- Some assets may not transfer (proprietary funds)
- Check for account closing fees
- Many brokers reimburse transfer fees

### In-Kind vs. Liquidation Transfer

**In-Kind:** Securities transfer as-is (no tax event)
**Liquidation:** Sell everything, transfer cash (triggers taxes)

Always prefer in-kind transfers to avoid unnecessary tax events.

## Step-Up in Basis

One significant advantage of taxable accounts: the step-up in basis at death.

### How It Works

When you die, your heirs receive investments at their current market value (not your original purchase price).

**Example:**
- You bought stock for $10,000
- At death, it's worth $100,000
- Your heir's cost basis: $100,000
- If they sell immediately, $0 capital gains tax
- $90,000 in gains never taxed

This is a powerful estate planning feature not available in retirement accounts.

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Excessive Trading

Each sale is a potential taxable event. Frequent trading:
- Creates short-term gains (high tax rate)
- Generates wash sale complications
- Incurs hidden costs (spreads, market impact)

### 2. Ignoring Tax-Loss Harvesting

Free money left on the table. Review positions annually for loss harvesting opportunities.

### 3. Poor Asset Location

Holding REITs and bonds in taxable accounts while stocks are in IRAs costs you in taxes.

### 4. Not Specifying Cost Basis

Letting broker default to FIFO when specific identification would save taxes.

### 5. Triggering Wash Sales

Buying back too soon or in another account (IRAs count too).

## Key Takeaways

- **Brokerage accounts offer flexibility** - No limits or restrictions, but no tax advantages
- **Cash accounts are safest** - Margin is risky and unnecessary for most investors
- **Commissions are mostly $0** - But watch for other fees and PFOF
- **Taxes matter significantly** - Long-term gains have preferential rates
- **Tax-loss harvesting saves money** - But watch wash sale rules
- **Asset location is powerful** - Put tax-inefficient assets in tax-advantaged accounts
- **Step-up in basis** - Significant estate planning benefit
