# Chapter 9: Health Savings Accounts (HSAs)

## Overview

A Health Savings Account (HSA) is the only account in the U.S. tax code with triple tax advantages: tax-deductible contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified expenses. Some financial experts consider it the best retirement account available for those who qualify.

This chapter covers HSA eligibility, contribution strategies, and how to use your HSA as a powerful retirement savings vehicle.

---

## The Triple Tax Advantage

### 1. Tax-Deductible Contributions

Contributions reduce your taxable income immediately—whether made through payroll deduction or directly. Payroll contributions also avoid FICA taxes (7.65% savings).

### 2. Tax-Free Growth

All investment earnings—dividends, interest, capital gains—grow completely tax-free.

### 3. Tax-Free Withdrawals

Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are 100% tax-free at any age.

### Comparison to Other Accounts

| Account | Contribution | Growth | Withdrawal |
|---------|--------------|--------|------------|
| Traditional 401(k)/IRA | Tax-deductible | Tax-deferred | Taxed |
| Roth 401(k)/IRA | After-tax | Tax-free | Tax-free |
| **HSA** | **Tax-deductible** | **Tax-free** | **Tax-free*** |

*For qualified medical expenses

No other account offers all three tax benefits.

## Eligibility Requirements

To contribute to an HSA, you must:

### 1. Have a High Deductible Health Plan (HDHP)

**2024 HDHP Requirements:**

| Coverage Type | Minimum Deductible | Maximum Out-of-Pocket |
|--------------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Individual | $1,600 | $8,050 |
| Family | $3,200 | $16,100 |

### 2. Have No Other Health Coverage

You cannot be covered by:
- Another non-HDHP health plan
- Medicare
- VA benefits (for non-service-connected issues)
- Flexible Spending Account (FSA) that covers medical expenses*

*Limited-purpose FSA (dental/vision only) is allowed

### 3. Not Be Claimed as a Dependent

If someone else can claim you as a dependent on their taxes, you can't contribute to an HSA.

### 4. Not Be Enrolled in Medicare

Once you enroll in Medicare (typically at 65), you can no longer contribute to an HSA. You can still use existing HSA funds.

---

## Contribution Limits (2024-2025)

| Coverage Type | 2024 | 2025 |
|--------------|------|------|
| Individual | $4,150 | $4,300 |
| Family | $8,300 | $8,550 |
| Age 55+ catch-up | $1,000 | $1,000 |

### Full-Year Contribution Rule

You can contribute the full annual limit if you're HSA-eligible on December 1st, even if you weren't eligible all year. However:

**Testing period:** You must remain HSA-eligible through December 31st of the following year, or face taxes and penalties on excess contributions.

### Employer Contributions

Employer HSA contributions count toward your limit. If your employer contributes $1,000 to your individual HSA, you can contribute $3,150 more.

## HSA as a Retirement Account

### The Stealth Strategy

Most people use their HSA to pay current medical bills. Smart investors use it differently:

1. **Pay medical expenses out-of-pocket** (using regular savings)
2. **Keep all receipts** indefinitely
3. **Invest HSA funds** for long-term growth
4. **Reimburse yourself years or decades later** (no time limit!)

### Why This Works

- There's no deadline to reimburse yourself for qualified expenses
- $500 medical bill in 2024 can be reimbursed from your HSA in 2044
- All the investment growth between now and then is tax-free

### Example

- Age 30: Start HSA, begin saving receipts
- Years 30-65: Contribute max, invest aggressively, pay medical bills from regular savings
- Age 65: HSA has grown to $500,000+
- Have $150,000 in accumulated medical receipts
- Withdraw $150,000 tax-free (reimburse old expenses)
- Remaining $350,000 can cover future medical costs tax-free

### After Age 65

If you withdraw HSA funds for non-medical expenses after age 65:
- No penalty
- Taxed as ordinary income (like Traditional IRA)

Before age 65, non-medical withdrawals face income tax PLUS 20% penalty.

## Qualified Medical Expenses

### What Qualifies

- Doctor visits and hospital stays
- Prescription medications
- Dental care (cleanings, fillings, braces, etc.)
- Vision care (glasses, contacts, LASIK)
- Mental health services
- Physical therapy
- Medical equipment (crutches, wheelchairs, etc.)
- Long-term care insurance premiums
- Medicare premiums (after 65)
- COBRA premiums

### What Doesn't Qualify

- Cosmetic procedures (without medical necessity)
- Over-the-counter medications (some exceptions)
- Health club memberships (usually)
- Non-prescription supplements (usually)

### Keep Your Receipts

Store receipts digitally (scan/photograph) organized by year. You may need to prove expenses were qualified if audited.

## Choosing an HSA Provider

### Employer HSA vs. External Provider

**Employer HSA:**
- Payroll deduction (saves FICA taxes)
- May have fees or limited investment options
- Convenient

**External HSA:**
- You choose provider with best features
- No FICA tax savings (unless transferred from employer HSA)
- Better investment options usually

**Strategy:** Contribute through employer (FICA savings), then transfer to better external provider periodically.

### Top HSA Providers for Investing

| Provider | Investment Threshold | Options | Fees |
|----------|---------------------|---------|------|
| Fidelity | $0 | Full brokerage | $0 |
| Lively | $0 | TD Ameritrade | $0 |
| HealthEquity | Varies | Vanguard funds | Varies |

**Fidelity** is often considered the best: no fees, no investment minimum, full brokerage access.

### What to Look For

1. **No monthly fees**
2. **No investment minimum** (or low minimum)
3. **Good investment options** (low-cost index funds)
4. **No transfer fees**
5. **Good mobile app**

## HSA Investment Strategy

### Cash vs. Invested

**Keep in cash:** 1-2 years of deductibles (emergency medical fund)
**Invest the rest:** Long-term growth for retirement

### Asset Allocation

Since HSA withdrawals for medical expenses are always tax-free, you can afford to be aggressive:

- **Growth stocks/funds** - Tax-free appreciation
- **Taxable bonds** - Interest would be taxed elsewhere
- **REITs** - Dividends would be taxed as ordinary income elsewhere

An aggressive allocation makes sense because you're likely not touching this money for decades.

### Sample HSA Portfolio

- 80% Total U.S. Stock Market (VTI or equivalent)
- 15% International Stocks (VXUS)
- 5% Cash (covers 1-2 years of deductible)

Adjust based on your overall portfolio allocation.

## HSA vs. FSA

| Feature | HSA | FSA |
|---------|-----|-----|
| Eligibility | HDHP required | Any employer plan |
| Contribution limit (2024) | $4,150/$8,300 | $3,050 |
| Rollover | Unlimited | $610 max (or forfeit) |
| Portability | Yours forever | Tied to employer |
| Investment | Yes | No |
| Age 65+ rule | Becomes like IRA | N/A |

**Bottom line:** If you qualify for HSA, it's almost always better than FSA.

## Common HSA Mistakes

### 1. Not Contributing the Maximum

HSA is one of the best tax-advantaged accounts. Max it out if possible.

### 2. Using HSA for Current Expenses

If you can afford to pay out-of-pocket, do so. Let HSA grow tax-free.

### 3. Not Investing HSA Funds

Keeping all funds in cash means missing out on decades of tax-free growth.

### 4. Losing Receipts

Keep documented records of all medical expenses. You may want to reimburse yourself years later.

### 5. Contributing While Ineligible

If you gain non-HDHP coverage or enroll in Medicare, you must stop contributing immediately (pro-rata limits apply for partial years).

### 6. Ignoring Employer Contributions

Remember employer contributions count toward your limit. Don't over-contribute.

## HSA and Medicare

### Important Deadline

You can no longer contribute to an HSA once enrolled in Medicare—including Medicare Part A.

**Social Security complication:** If you claim Social Security after age 65, you're automatically enrolled in Medicare Part A (retroactive up to 6 months).

**Strategy:** If you want to maximize HSA contributions, delay Social Security until you're done contributing to HSA, or stop HSA contributions 6 months before claiming Social Security.

### Using HSA Funds in Retirement

After 65, you can use HSA funds for:
- Medicare premiums (Parts B, C, D) - Tax-free
- Long-term care premiums - Tax-free (with limits)
- Out-of-pocket medical expenses - Tax-free
- Any purpose - Taxed as ordinary income (no penalty)

Medical expenses in retirement are substantial. Having a tax-free pool of money for healthcare is extremely valuable.

## HSA Contribution Priority

Where does HSA fit in the optimal contribution order?

**Recommended priority:**
1. 401(k) up to employer match
2. **Max HSA**
3. Max Roth IRA
4. Max 401(k)
5. Taxable brokerage

Some experts even prioritize HSA above Roth IRA due to the triple tax advantage.

## The HSA Math

### 25-Year Example

- Annual contribution: $4,000
- Investment return: 8%
- Years: 25

**Result:** ~$315,000 in tax-free money for medical expenses

Assuming average medical costs in retirement of $150,000-$300,000 per person, your HSA could cover a significant portion tax-free.

### Value of Triple Tax Advantage

If you're in the 24% tax bracket and 8% state tax (32% total):

| Account Type | $4,000 Contribution | After 25 Years @ 8% | After-Tax Value |
|--------------|--------------------|--------------------|-----------------|
| Taxable | $2,720 (after tax) | $18,500 | ~$15,700 (cap gains tax) |
| Traditional IRA | $4,000 | $27,300 | ~$18,500 (income tax) |
| Roth IRA | $2,720 (after tax)* | $18,500 | $18,500 |
| **HSA** | **$4,000** | **$27,300** | **$27,300** |

*Assuming you invest the same after-tax amount

The HSA provides approximately 50% more after-tax value than a Roth IRA over this period.

## Key Takeaways

- **HSA is triple tax-advantaged** - Deductible, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals
- **Requires HDHP** - Must meet minimum deductible requirements
- **Best used as stealth retirement account** - Pay out-of-pocket, invest HSA, reimburse later
- **Keep receipts forever** - No time limit on reimbursement
- **Invest for growth** - Don't leave all funds in cash
- **After 65, becomes like Traditional IRA** - Non-medical withdrawals just taxed (no penalty)
- **Stop contributing before Medicare** - Can't contribute once enrolled
