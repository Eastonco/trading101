## Overview

The stock market can seem mysterious and intimidating, but at its core, it's simply a marketplace where buyers and sellers exchange ownership in companies. Understanding how this market works—from the mechanics of a trade to the role of different participants—demystifies what many consider a complex financial system.

This chapter explains what stocks are, how exchanges operate, who participates in the market, and what actually happens when you click "buy."

---

## What Is a Stock?

### Ownership in a Company

A stock (also called a share or equity) represents partial ownership in a company. When you buy a stock, you're buying a tiny piece of that business.

**Example:** Apple Inc. has approximately 15.5 billion shares outstanding. If you buy 100 shares, you own about 0.0000006% of Apple. Small? Yes. But you still have legitimate ownership rights.

### What Ownership Means

As a shareholder, you're entitled to:

1. **Voting rights** - Vote on major company decisions (board elections, mergers)
2. **Dividends** - Share of company profits (if the company pays them)
3. **Capital appreciation** - Benefit if the stock price increases
4. **Claim on assets** - If the company liquidates, shareholders get paid (after creditors)

### Types of Stock

**Common Stock**
- Standard ownership shares
- Voting rights (usually one vote per share)
- Dividends are variable (if paid at all)
- Last in line during bankruptcy

**Preferred Stock**
- Hybrid between stocks and bonds
- Fixed dividend payments
- Priority over common stock for dividends and liquidation
- Usually no voting rights
- Less price appreciation potential

Most individual investors buy common stock.

## Why Companies Issue Stock

### Raising Capital

Companies issue stock to raise money without taking on debt. This is called an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)** when done for the first time.

**Example:** When a company IPOs, it might sell 10 million shares at $20 each, raising $200 million. This money can fund expansion, research, acquisitions, or debt repayment.

### The Trade-Off

By issuing stock, existing owners (founders, early investors) dilute their ownership. If you owned 50% of a company before an IPO and the IPO doubles the total shares, you now own 25%.

Why do it? Because 25% of a well-funded, growing company can be worth far more than 50% of an underfunded one.

### Primary vs. Secondary Markets

**Primary Market:** When companies issue new shares directly to investors (IPO, secondary offerings). The company receives the money.

**Secondary Market:** Where investors trade existing shares with each other. When you buy Apple stock, you're buying from another investor—Apple doesn't receive anything. This is what most people mean by "the stock market."

## How Stock Exchanges Work

### What Is an Exchange?

A stock exchange is an organized marketplace that facilitates buying and selling of securities. Think of it as a farmers market for stocks—a central location where buyers and sellers meet.

### Major U.S. Exchanges

**New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)**
- Largest exchange by market capitalization
- Founded in 1792
- Physical trading floor on Wall Street (plus electronic trading)
- Known for large, established companies
- Examples: Walmart, JPMorgan, Coca-Cola

**Nasdaq**
- Second-largest exchange globally
- Fully electronic (no physical trading floor)
- Known for technology and growth companies
- Examples: Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Google

**Other Exchanges**
- CBOE (Chicago Board Options Exchange) - Options trading
- BATS/IEX - Electronic exchanges competing for volume
- OTC Markets - Smaller companies not on major exchanges

### Trading Hours

**Regular Session:** 9:30 AM - 4:00 PM Eastern Time (Monday-Friday)

**Extended Hours:**
- Pre-market: 4:00 AM - 9:30 AM
- After-hours: 4:00 PM - 8:00 PM

Extended hours have lower volume and wider spreads—most retail investors should stick to regular hours.

## Market Participants

Understanding who else is in the market helps you understand price movements.

### Retail Investors

Individual investors like you. Historically a small portion of volume, though growing due to commission-free trading apps.

**Characteristics:**
- Smaller trade sizes
- More emotional decision-making
- Less sophisticated analysis
- Often trend-followers

### Institutional Investors

Large organizations managing significant capital.

**Types:**
- **Mutual funds** - Pool money from many investors
- **Pension funds** - Manage retirement assets
- **Hedge funds** - Aggressive strategies for wealthy investors
- **Insurance companies** - Invest premium float
- **Endowments** - University and foundation money

**Impact:** Institutions represent 70-80% of daily trading volume. Their moves significantly impact prices.

### Market Makers

Firms that provide liquidity by always offering to buy and sell. They profit from the bid-ask spread.

**Example:** A market maker might offer to buy a stock at $49.95 and sell at $50.05. They pocket the $0.10 spread on each round-trip trade.

### High-Frequency Traders (HFT)

Algorithmic traders using sophisticated technology to trade in milliseconds. They account for roughly 50% of volume.

**What they do:**
- Arbitrage tiny price differences across exchanges
- Provide liquidity
- Controversial: some argue they front-run other traders

### Brokers

Intermediaries that execute trades on your behalf. When you use Fidelity, Schwab, or Robinhood, you're using a broker.

## How a Trade Actually Works

Let's walk through what happens when you buy 100 shares of a company.

### Step 1: You Place an Order

You tell your broker you want to buy 100 shares. You specify:
- **Market order:** Buy at the current best available price
- **Limit order:** Buy only if the price is at or below your specified price
- **Stop order:** Buy if price reaches a certain level

### Step 2: Order Routing

Your broker routes your order to an exchange or market maker. Some brokers receive "payment for order flow" for directing orders to specific market makers—this is why many brokers are "commission-free."

### Step 3: Matching

Your buy order is matched with a seller's sell order. If you're buying 100 shares at market price and someone is selling 100 shares at $50.05, you're matched.

### Step 4: Execution

The trade is executed. You now own the shares (pending settlement). This happens in milliseconds for liquid stocks.

### Step 5: Settlement

The actual exchange of money for shares occurs. Standard settlement is T+1 (trade date plus one business day). Until settlement, the trade is technically pending.

### Step 6: Confirmation

You receive a confirmation showing:
- Number of shares
- Price per share
- Total cost
- Fees (if any)
- Settlement date

## Understanding Stock Prices

### What Determines Price?

At the most basic level: **supply and demand.**

If more people want to buy a stock than sell it, the price rises. If more people want to sell than buy, the price falls.

### What Drives Supply and Demand?

**Company factors:**
- Earnings reports
- Revenue growth
- New products or services
- Management changes
- Competitive position

**Economic factors:**
- Interest rates
- Inflation
- GDP growth
- Unemployment

**Market factors:**
- Overall market sentiment
- Sector trends
- Index inclusion/exclusion

**External factors:**
- Regulatory changes
- Geopolitical events
- Natural disasters

### The Bid-Ask Spread

**Bid:** The highest price buyers are willing to pay
**Ask:** The lowest price sellers are willing to accept
**Spread:** The difference between bid and ask

**Example:**
- Bid: $49.95
- Ask: $50.00
- Spread: $0.05

Liquid stocks (like Apple) have penny-wide spreads. Illiquid stocks might have spreads of $0.50 or more.

### Market vs. Limit Orders

**Market Order:**
- Executes immediately at best available price
- You get the shares but may pay more than expected
- Use for liquid stocks when speed matters

**Limit Order:**
- Executes only at your specified price or better
- May not execute if price doesn't reach your limit
- Use for less liquid stocks or when price precision matters

**Example:** Stock shows $50.00. You place a market order for 1,000 shares. But there are only 500 shares available at $50.00—the rest fill at $50.10. This is called "slippage."

## Market Capitalization

Market cap indicates a company's total value in the stock market.

**Formula:** Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding

### Size Categories

| Category | Market Cap | Examples |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| Mega Cap | $200B+ | Apple, Microsoft, Amazon |
| Large Cap | $10B-$200B | Nike, Starbucks, Target |
| Mid Cap | $2B-$10B | Crocs, Five Below |
| Small Cap | $300M-$2B | Many regional companies |
| Micro Cap | Under $300M | Speculative, higher risk |

### Why Size Matters

- **Large caps:** More stable, more liquid, slower growth
- **Small caps:** More volatile, less liquid, higher growth potential
- **Diversification:** Owning different sizes reduces risk

## Bull Markets and Bear Markets

### Bull Market

A sustained period of rising prices, typically defined as a 20%+ gain from recent lows.

**Characteristics:**
- Optimism and confidence
- Strong economic conditions
- Increasing participation
- "Buy the dip" mentality

### Bear Market

A sustained decline, typically defined as a 20%+ drop from recent highs.

**Characteristics:**
- Fear and pessimism
- Economic concerns
- Reduced participation
- "Sell the rally" mentality

### Historical Perspective

Since 1926, the U.S. stock market has spent roughly:
- 78% of the time in bull markets
- 22% of the time in bear markets

Average bull market: 6 years, +339% gain
Average bear market: 1.3 years, -36% decline

Long-term investors benefit from staying invested through both.

## Common Pitfalls for New Investors

### 1. Confusing Price with Value

A $10 stock isn't "cheaper" than a $500 stock. What matters is what you get for your money (earnings, assets, growth) relative to the price.

### 2. Thinking You Need to Pick Individual Stocks

Most people are better served by diversified index funds than by trying to pick winning stocks.

### 3. Panic Selling During Downturns

The market has always recovered from crashes—eventually. Selling at the bottom locks in losses.

### 4. Checking Prices Too Often

Daily price movements are noise. Obsessive checking leads to emotional decisions.

### 5. Following "Hot Tips"

By the time investment ideas reach you through social media or friends, they're usually played out.

## Key Takeaways

- **Stocks represent ownership** - You're buying a piece of a real business
- **Exchanges facilitate trading** - NYSE and Nasdaq are the main U.S. venues
- **Many participants, different goals** - Institutions dominate volume; understand who you're trading against
- **Price = supply and demand** - Influenced by countless factors, impossible to predict short-term
- **Market orders vs. limit orders** - Know the difference; limit orders offer price protection
- **Market cap matters** - Different size companies have different characteristics
- **Bull and bear markets are normal** - Both are temporary; stay invested through both
