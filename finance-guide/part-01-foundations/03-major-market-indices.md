# Chapter 3: Major Market Indices

## Overview

When someone asks "How's the market doing today?", they're usually asking about an index. Market indices are benchmarks that track the performance of a group of stocks, giving us a snapshot of how a particular segment of the market is performing.

Understanding the major indices—what they contain, how they're calculated, and what they represent—is essential for any investor. This chapter breaks down the most important benchmarks you'll encounter.

---

## What Is a Market Index?

An index is a hypothetical portfolio of stocks designed to represent a particular market or segment. You can't invest directly in an index, but you can invest in index funds that track them.

### Purpose of Indices

1. **Benchmarking** - Measure your portfolio's performance against the market
2. **Market health indicator** - Quick snapshot of overall market conditions
3. **Investment vehicles** - Index funds and ETFs track these benchmarks
4. **Economic indicator** - Stock markets often reflect economic expectations

### Index Weighting Methods

How an index weights its components dramatically affects its behavior.

**Price-Weighted:** Higher-priced stocks have more influence
- Example: Dow Jones Industrial Average
- A $200 stock moves the index twice as much as a $100 stock
- Stock splits change the weighting

**Market-Cap Weighted:** Larger companies have more influence
- Example: S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite
- A $3 trillion company affects the index more than a $30 billion company
- Most common method

**Equal-Weighted:** Every stock has the same influence
- Example: S&P 500 Equal Weight Index
- 500 stocks each represent 0.2% of the index
- Smaller companies have more influence than in cap-weighted

## The S&P 500

### What It Is

The Standard & Poor's 500 is the most widely followed benchmark for U.S. large-cap stocks. It contains 500 of the largest publicly traded companies in the United States.

### Key Characteristics

- **Number of stocks:** Approximately 500 (can fluctuate slightly)
- **Weighting:** Market-cap weighted
- **Selection:** Committee-selected based on criteria (size, liquidity, profitability)
- **Rebalanced:** Quarterly

### What It Represents

The S&P 500 represents approximately 80% of the total U.S. stock market by value. When people talk about "the market," they usually mean the S&P 500.

## The Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA)

### What It Is

The Dow is the oldest and most recognized stock market index, tracking 30 large, blue-chip American companies. Despite its fame, it's actually a poor representation of the overall market.

### Key Characteristics

- **Number of stocks:** 30
- **Weighting:** Price-weighted (unique among major indices)
- **Selection:** Committee-selected, focuses on industry leaders
- **History:** Created in 1896

### The Price-Weighting Problem

Because the Dow is price-weighted, a stock's influence depends on its share price, not its company size.

**Example:**
- UnitedHealth ($500/share) has more influence than Apple ($180/share)
- Even though Apple is worth 10x more as a company

This makes the Dow a quirky, somewhat arbitrary measure of market health.

### Current Components (Selected)

| Company | Industry |
|---------|----------|
| Apple | Technology |
| Microsoft | Technology |
| UnitedHealth | Healthcare |
| Goldman Sachs | Finance |
| Home Depot | Retail |
| Caterpillar | Industrial |
| Boeing | Aerospace |
| Coca-Cola | Consumer |
| Nike | Consumer |
| Visa | Finance |

### Why It Still Matters

Despite its limitations, the Dow remains widely quoted because:
- Brand recognition (129 years old)
- Simple to understand (30 stocks)
- Historical data going back over a century
- Media loves round numbers ("Dow breaks 40,000!")

### Historical Performance

The Dow has averaged approximately 7-8% annually over its history (lower than S&P 500 due to different composition and weighting).

### How to Invest

- **DIA** (SPDR Dow Jones Industrial Average ETF) - 0.16% expense ratio

## The Nasdaq Composite

### What It Is

The Nasdaq Composite tracks all stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange—over 3,000 companies. It's heavily weighted toward technology and growth stocks.

### Key Characteristics

- **Number of stocks:** 3,000+
- **Weighting:** Market-cap weighted
- **Focus:** Technology, biotech, growth companies
- **Exchange-based:** Includes everything on Nasdaq

### Why It's Tech-Heavy

Major tech companies chose to list on Nasdaq (originally an electronic exchange) rather than NYSE. This historical accident means the Nasdaq is essentially a tech index.

**Tech Concentration:** Technology stocks represent approximately 50%+ of the index by weight.

### Nasdaq vs. S&P 500

| Characteristic | Nasdaq Composite | S&P 500 |
|----------------|------------------|---------|
| # of stocks | 3,000+ | 500 |
| Tech weight | ~50% | ~29% |
| Volatility | Higher | Lower |
| International | Some | U.S. only |

### Historical Performance

The Nasdaq tends to outperform during tech booms and underperform during tech busts.

- 1999 (dot-com peak): +86%
- 2000-2002 (dot-com bust): -78%
- 2020: +44%
- 2022 (tech selloff): -33%

### How to Invest

- **QQQ** (Invesco Nasdaq 100 ETF) - Tracks top 100 non-financial Nasdaq stocks
- Not many funds track the full Nasdaq Composite due to its size

## The Nasdaq 100

### What It Is

The Nasdaq 100 is a subset of the Nasdaq Composite, containing the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq. It's the index that QQQ tracks.

### Key Characteristics

- **Number of stocks:** 100
- **Weighting:** Modified market-cap weighted
- **Excludes:** Financial companies
- **Rebalanced:** Annually, with quarterly adjustments

### Top Holdings (2024)

| Company | Weight |
|---------|--------|
| Apple | ~11% |
| Microsoft | ~10% |
| Nvidia | ~8% |
| Amazon | ~6% |
| Broadcom | ~5% |
| Meta | ~4% |
| Google | ~4% |
| Costco | ~3% |
| Tesla | ~3% |

**Concentration:** The top 10 stocks represent roughly 50% of the index.

### Nasdaq 100 vs. S&P 500

For many investors, the choice between QQQ (Nasdaq 100) and SPY/VOO (S&P 500) is a key decision.

**QQQ Advantages:**
- Higher growth potential
- More tech exposure
- Has historically outperformed during tech bull markets

**QQQ Disadvantages:**
- Higher volatility
- Less diversification
- Underperforms when tech struggles

**2010-2020 returns:**
- Nasdaq 100: +420%
- S&P 500: +255%

**2022 returns:**
- Nasdaq 100: -33%
- S&P 500: -19%

## The Russell Indices

### Russell 1000

Tracks the 1,000 largest U.S. stocks by market cap. Covers approximately 93% of the investable U.S. equity market. Similar to but broader than the S&P 500.

### Russell 2000

The benchmark for U.S. small-cap stocks. Tracks stocks ranked 1,001-3,000 by market cap.

**Key Characteristics:**
- ~2,000 stocks
- Average market cap: ~$2-3 billion
- More volatile than large caps
- Often leads during economic recoveries

### Russell 3000

Combines Russell 1000 + Russell 2000. Represents approximately 98% of the investable U.S. stock market.

### Why Russell 2000 Matters

Small-cap stocks behave differently than large caps:
- More sensitive to domestic economy
- Less analyst coverage (potential for mispricing)
- Higher growth potential but higher risk
- Often outperform coming out of recessions

### How to Invest

- **IWM** (iShares Russell 2000 ETF) - Small caps
- **IWB** (iShares Russell 1000 ETF) - Large caps
- **IWV** (iShares Russell 3000 ETF) - Total market

## Total Market Indices

### What They Are

Total market indices aim to capture the entire investable stock market rather than just a segment.

### VTI / Vanguard Total Stock Market

- **Tracks:** CRSP US Total Market Index
- **Holdings:** 4,000+ stocks
- **Coverage:** ~100% of investable U.S. market
- **Expense ratio:** 0.03%

### Why Choose Total Market

**Advantages over S&P 500:**
- Includes mid and small caps
- More diversification
- Captures the entire market

**Practical difference:** Returns are very similar to S&P 500 because large caps dominate both. Over 10+ year periods, the difference is typically 0.1-0.3% annually.

## International Indices

### MSCI EAFE

**Europe, Australasia, and Far East** - Developed markets excluding U.S. and Canada.

- ~800 stocks
- Major countries: Japan, UK, France, Germany, Australia
- Used for international developed market allocation

**ETF:** EFA (iShares MSCI EAFE)

### MSCI Emerging Markets

Tracks stocks in developing economies.

- ~1,400 stocks
- Major countries: China, Taiwan, India, South Korea, Brazil
- Higher growth potential, higher risk

**ETF:** EEM or VWO (Vanguard)

### MSCI ACWI (All Country World Index)

Global stocks, both developed and emerging markets. Approximately:
- 60% U.S.
- 28% International Developed
- 12% Emerging Markets

**ETF:** ACWI (iShares)

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Thinking the Dow Represents "The Market"

The Dow's 30 stocks and price-weighting make it a poor market proxy. Use S&P 500 or total market indices.

### 2. Chasing Last Year's Best Performer

Indices rotate leadership. Tech (Nasdaq) dominated 2010-2021; it may not dominate the next decade.

### 3. Ignoring Concentration Risk

S&P 500's top 10 stocks represent 30%+ of the index. You're more concentrated than you might think.

### 4. Over-Complicating Allocation

For most investors, a simple S&P 500 or total market fund captures the vast majority of U.S. stock market returns.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **S&P 500 is the most important benchmark** - 500 large U.S. companies, ~80% of market value
- **The Dow is outdated but famous** - 30 stocks, price-weighted, poor market representation
- **Nasdaq is tech-heavy** - Great during tech booms, painful during tech busts
- **Russell 2000 tracks small caps** - Different behavior, higher volatility, domestic focus
- **Total market captures everything** - Practical returns similar to S&P 500
- **International indices exist** - Consider EAFE and Emerging Markets for diversification
