# Chapter 5: Economic Indicators & Market Drivers

## Overview

Stock prices don't move in a vacuum. They respond to economic conditions, Federal Reserve policy, corporate earnings, and investor sentiment. Understanding these drivers helps you contextualize market movements and avoid panic during volatility.

This chapter covers the key economic indicators investors watch, how the Federal Reserve influences markets, and the relationship between the economy and stock prices.

---

## The Economy and the Stock Market

### They're Related But Not the Same

A common misconception: the stock market equals the economy. It doesn't.

**The economy** = Total production, employment, and spending in a country
**The stock market** = Prices of publicly traded companies based on expected future profits

Key differences:

1. **Stock market looks forward** - Prices reflect expectations 6-18 months ahead
2. **Timing mismatch** - Markets often bottom before recessions end
3. **Different composition** - S&P 500 companies aren't representative of the whole economy
4. **Global exposure** - Many U.S. companies earn profits abroad

### Why Markets Sometimes Seem Disconnected

During COVID-19 (2020), unemployment hit 14.7% while the stock market was near all-time highs. Why?

- Markets anticipated recovery
- Tech companies (large S&P weight) thrived
- Federal Reserve injected massive liquidity
- Low interest rates made stocks relatively attractive

The lesson: markets discount the future, not the present.

## Key Economic Indicators

### Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

**What it is:** Total value of goods and services produced in a country over a period (usually quarterly).

**Why it matters:** The broadest measure of economic health. Growing GDP = expanding economy.

**How it's measured:**
- GDP = Consumption + Investment + Government Spending + Net Exports
- Reported quarterly, with revisions

**Key thresholds:**
- 2-3% growth: Healthy economy
- 0% or negative: Recessionary
- Two consecutive negative quarters: Technical recession

**Market impact:** Strong GDP = bullish for stocks (usually), but can signal higher interest rates.

### Employment Data

**Nonfarm Payrolls (Monthly Jobs Report)**

Released first Friday of each month, this is one of the most market-moving reports.

- **What it measures:** Jobs added/lost in the previous month
- **Healthy range:** 100,000-250,000 jobs added monthly
- **Includes:** Unemployment rate, average hourly earnings, labor force participation

**Unemployment Rate**

| Rate | Interpretation |
|------|----------------|
| 3-4% | Full employment |
| 4-5% | Healthy |
| 6-7% | Moderate concern |
| 8%+ | Serious weakness |

**Market impact:**
- Too strong = Fed may raise rates (can be negative for stocks)
- Too weak = Economic concern (negative for stocks)
- Goldilocks (moderate) = Best for markets

**Weekly Jobless Claims**

More timely than monthly data.
- **Initial claims:** New unemployment filings
- **Continuing claims:** Ongoing unemployment
- **Under 250,000 initial claims:** Healthy labor market

### Inflation Indicators

**Consumer Price Index (CPI)**

Measures price changes for a basket of consumer goods and services.

- **Headline CPI:** All items including food and energy
- **Core CPI:** Excludes volatile food and energy
- **Released:** Monthly

**Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE)**

The Fed's preferred inflation measure.
- Broader than CPI
- Core PCE is the Fed's primary target

**Producer Price Index (PPI)**

Measures prices at the wholesale/producer level. Can be a leading indicator of CPI.

**Key thresholds:**

| Inflation Rate | Interpretation |
|----------------|----------------|
| 0% or negative | Deflation (dangerous) |
| 1-2% | Below target |
| 2% | Fed's target |
| 2-3% | Acceptable |
| 4%+ | Concerning |
| 6%+ | High inflation |

**Market impact:** High inflation = Fed raises rates = potentially negative for stocks. Deflation = economic weakness concerns.

### Consumer Confidence & Sentiment

**Consumer Confidence Index (Conference Board)**
**University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment**

These measure how optimistic consumers feel about the economy and their finances.

**Why it matters:** Consumer spending is ~70% of U.S. GDP. Confident consumers spend more.

**Market impact:** Strong sentiment = economic optimism = positive for stocks (usually).

### Manufacturing and Business Activity

**ISM Manufacturing PMI (Purchasing Managers Index)**

Monthly survey of manufacturing executives.

| Reading | Interpretation |
|---------|----------------|
| Above 50 | Manufacturing expanding |
| Below 50 | Manufacturing contracting |
| Above 55 | Strong expansion |
| Below 45 | Serious contraction |

**ISM Services PMI**

Same concept for the services sector (larger part of U.S. economy).

**Industrial Production**

Measures output from manufacturing, mining, and utilities.

### Housing Market Indicators

Housing is a leading economic indicator and major wealth driver for consumers.

**Key metrics:**
- **Housing Starts:** New construction begun
- **Building Permits:** Approved future construction
- **Existing Home Sales:** Volume of home transactions
- **New Home Sales:** Newly built home sales
- **Case-Shiller Index:** Home prices

**Market impact:** Strong housing = consumer confidence, construction jobs, mortgage activity.

## The Federal Reserve and Interest Rates

### What Is the Federal Reserve?

The Fed is the U.S. central bank, responsible for:

1. **Monetary policy** - Managing interest rates and money supply
2. **Maximum employment** - Dual mandate goal
3. **Stable prices** - 2% inflation target
4. **Financial system stability** - Lender of last resort

### The Federal Funds Rate

The Fed's primary policy tool is the **federal funds rate**—the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans.

**How it works:**
- Fed sets a target range (e.g., 5.25%-5.50%)
- This rate influences all other interest rates
- Mortgages, car loans, savings accounts, bonds all adjust

### Fed Policy Actions

**Rate Hikes (Tightening)**
- Used to fight inflation
- Makes borrowing more expensive
- Slows economic activity
- Generally negative for stock prices short-term

**Rate Cuts (Easing)**
- Used to stimulate the economy
- Makes borrowing cheaper
- Encourages spending and investment
- Generally positive for stock prices

**Quantitative Easing (QE)**
- Fed buys bonds to inject money into the system
- Pushes down long-term interest rates
- Used during severe downturns (2008-2014, 2020-2022)

**Quantitative Tightening (QT)**
- Fed lets bonds roll off its balance sheet
- Removes money from the system
- Tightens financial conditions

### How Interest Rates Affect Stocks

**Higher rates hurt stocks because:**
1. Bonds become more attractive alternatives
2. Future earnings are worth less (higher discount rate)
3. Companies' borrowing costs increase
4. Consumer spending decreases

**Lower rates help stocks because:**
1. Bonds offer less competition ("TINA" - There Is No Alternative)
2. Future earnings are worth more (lower discount rate)
3. Companies can borrow cheaply
4. Consumers spend more

### The Yield Curve

The yield curve plots interest rates across different bond maturities.

**Normal yield curve:** Long-term rates higher than short-term (reward for locking up money longer)

**Inverted yield curve:** Short-term rates higher than long-term

**Why inversion matters:** An inverted yield curve has preceded every U.S. recession since 1955. It signals that markets expect the Fed to cut rates in the future (due to economic weakness).

**2s/10s Spread:** The difference between 2-year and 10-year Treasury yields is the most-watched curve measure.

## Corporate Earnings

### Why Earnings Matter

Ultimately, stock prices follow earnings over time. Companies that grow profits see their stock prices rise. Companies with declining profits see stock prices fall.

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)

**Formula:** Net Income ÷ Shares Outstanding

**Example:**
- Company earns $1 billion
- 500 million shares outstanding
- EPS = $2.00

### Earnings Season

Public companies report quarterly earnings (10-Q filings) and annual earnings (10-K filings).

**Earnings season timing:**
- Q1 earnings: April-May
- Q2 earnings: July-August
- Q3 earnings: October-November
- Q4 earnings: January-February

### Expectations Game

Stock prices react to earnings **relative to expectations**, not absolute numbers.

| Result | Typical Reaction |
|--------|------------------|
| Beat estimates | Stock rises |
| Miss estimates | Stock falls |
| Meet estimates | Muted reaction |
| Beat, but lower guidance | Often falls |

**Earnings surprises can cause significant price moves:**
- Positive surprise: +5% to +20% moves are common
- Negative surprise: -5% to -30% drops possible

### Forward Guidance

Management's outlook for future quarters often matters more than the current quarter's results. "Beat and raise" (beat estimates and raise guidance) is the best outcome.

## Market Sentiment Indicators

### VIX (Volatility Index)

Often called the "fear index," VIX measures expected volatility in the S&P 500 over the next 30 days.

| VIX Level | Interpretation |
|-----------|----------------|
| Under 15 | Low volatility, complacency |
| 15-20 | Normal |
| 20-30 | Elevated concern |
| 30-40 | High fear |
| 40+ | Extreme panic |

**Contrarian signal:** Extremely high VIX often marks market bottoms. Extremely low VIX can precede corrections.

### Put/Call Ratio

Measures the volume of put options (bearish bets) relative to call options (bullish bets).

| Ratio | Interpretation |
|-------|----------------|
| Under 0.7 | Bullish (potentially too bullish) |
| 0.7-1.0 | Neutral |
| Over 1.0 | Bearish (potentially oversold) |

### AAII Sentiment Survey

Weekly survey of individual investors' bullish/bearish/neutral stance.

**Contrarian indicator:** When sentiment is extremely bearish, markets often rally (and vice versa).

## The Business Cycle

### Four Phases

**1. Expansion**
- Growing GDP
- Falling unemployment
- Rising corporate profits
- Bull market in stocks

**2. Peak**
- Maximum economic activity
- Low unemployment
- Rising inflation
- Fed raising rates
- Market euphoria

**3. Contraction (Recession)**
- Declining GDP
- Rising unemployment
- Falling profits
- Bear market
- Fed cuts rates

**4. Trough**
- Minimum economic activity
- High unemployment
- Maximum pessimism
- Best buying opportunity (in hindsight)

### Sector Rotation

Different sectors perform better at different cycle phases:

| Cycle Phase | Strong Sectors | Weak Sectors |
|-------------|----------------|--------------|
| Early Expansion | Financials, Consumer Discretionary | Utilities, Healthcare |
| Late Expansion | Technology, Industrials | Utilities, Consumer Staples |
| Recession | Utilities, Consumer Staples, Healthcare | Financials, Industrials |
| Recovery | Consumer Discretionary, Industrials | Utilities |

## Global Factors

### Currency Effects

A strong U.S. dollar:
- Hurts U.S. exporters (products more expensive abroad)
- Hurts U.S. multinationals (foreign earnings worth less)
- Helps U.S. consumers (imports cheaper)

A weak dollar has opposite effects.

### International Events

Markets react to global events:
- Foreign central bank policies
- Geopolitical conflicts
- Trade agreements/wars
- Global supply chain disruptions
- Commodity price shocks (oil, etc.)

### Correlations

During crises, correlations increase—diversification benefits decrease exactly when you need them most. Global markets often fall together during severe stress.

## Putting It All Together

### Economic Calendar

Smart investors track the economic calendar. Key dates include:
- Monthly jobs report (first Friday)
- CPI release (mid-month)
- Fed meetings (8 per year)
- Quarterly GDP releases
- Earnings season dates

### Don't Try to Trade the News

While understanding economic factors is valuable, trying to trade short-term reactions to news is extremely difficult. By the time you see the news, markets have often already moved.

**Better approach:**
- Understand the big picture
- Maintain appropriate long-term allocation
- Don't panic during volatility
- Use downturns as buying opportunities
- Stay invested through cycles

---

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Conflating Economy with Markets

The economy and stock market move together long-term but can diverge significantly short-term.

### 2. Overreacting to Single Data Points

One report doesn't make a trend. Look for persistent patterns.

### 3. Fighting the Fed

"Don't fight the Fed" is investing wisdom. When the Fed is easing, it's usually good for stocks. When tightening, be more cautious.

### 4. Prediction Addiction

Economists are notoriously bad at predicting recessions. Base your strategy on long-term allocation, not economic forecasts.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **The stock market is forward-looking** - Prices reflect expectations, not current conditions
- **GDP, employment, and inflation are the big three** - These drive Fed policy
- **The Fed moves markets** - Interest rate decisions significantly impact valuations
- **Earnings drive long-term returns** - Corporate profits ultimately matter most
- **Sentiment is a contrarian indicator** - Extreme fear/greed often marks turning points
- **Business cycles are inevitable** - Expansions end; recessions end
- **Stay invested through cycles** - Timing the market is nearly impossible
