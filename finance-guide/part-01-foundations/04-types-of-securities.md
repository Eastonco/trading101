# Chapter 4: Types of Securities

## Overview

Securities are tradable financial assets. While "investing" often brings stocks to mind, there's a diverse universe of investment vehicles, each with different characteristics, risks, and purposes in a portfolio.

This chapter covers the main types of securities you'll encounter: stocks, bonds, ETFs, mutual funds, and REITs. Understanding each type helps you build a diversified portfolio that matches your goals.

---

## Stocks (Equities)

Covered in detail in Chapters 2 and 3, stocks represent ownership in a company.

### Quick Review

- **What you own:** Partial ownership of a business
- **Returns from:** Price appreciation + dividends
- **Risk level:** Medium to high
- **Best for:** Long-term growth

### Key Stock Categories

**By Size:**
- Large-cap: $10B+ market cap
- Mid-cap: $2B-$10B
- Small-cap: Under $2B

**By Style:**
- Growth: High growth expectations, often no dividends
- Value: Undervalued relative to fundamentals
- Blend: Mix of both

**By Geography:**
- Domestic: U.S. companies
- International developed: Europe, Japan, Australia
- Emerging markets: China, India, Brazil

**By Sector:**
11 GICS sectors (Technology, Healthcare, Financials, etc.)

## Bonds (Fixed Income)

Bonds are loans you make to governments or corporations. They promise to pay you back the principal plus interest.

### How Bonds Work

When you buy a bond, you're lending money to the issuer. In return, you receive:

1. **Coupon payments:** Regular interest payments (usually semi-annually)
2. **Par value:** Return of principal at maturity

**Example:**
- You buy a $10,000 bond with a 5% coupon and 10-year maturity
- You receive $500/year (5% of $10,000) in interest
- After 10 years, you get your $10,000 back
- Total received: $10,000 + $5,000 interest = $15,000

### Bond Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Par Value | Face value of the bond ($1,000 typical) |
| Coupon Rate | Annual interest rate |
| Maturity | When the bond expires |
| Yield | Your actual return based on purchase price |
| Credit Rating | Assessment of default risk |

### Types of Bonds

**Government Bonds**

*Treasury Securities (U.S. Government):*
- **T-Bills:** Mature in under 1 year, no coupon (sold at discount)
- **T-Notes:** 2-10 year maturities, semi-annual coupon
- **T-Bonds:** 20-30 year maturities, semi-annual coupon
- **TIPS:** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, adjust for inflation
- **I-Bonds:** Savings bonds with inflation adjustment

*Risk:* Effectively zero default risk for U.S. Treasuries

*Municipal Bonds:*
- Issued by state and local governments
- Interest often tax-free at federal (and sometimes state) level
- Attractive for high-income investors

**Corporate Bonds**

*Investment Grade:* BBB or higher rating
- Lower yields than junk bonds
- Lower default risk
- Examples: Apple, Microsoft, Johnson & Johnson bonds

*High Yield (Junk) Bonds:* BB or lower rating
- Higher yields to compensate for risk
- Higher default probability
- Behave more like stocks during market stress

### Bond Prices and Interest Rates

**Critical concept:** Bond prices move inversely to interest rates.

When interest rates rise:
- Existing bonds (with lower rates) become less attractive
- Their prices fall

When interest rates fall:
- Existing bonds (with higher rates) become more attractive
- Their prices rise

**Example:**
- You own a bond paying 4%
- New bonds are issued paying 5%
- Why would anyone buy your 4% bond at face value?
- Your bond's price must fall until its yield matches the market

### Duration: Measuring Interest Rate Sensitivity

Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to interest rate changes.

**Rule of thumb:** If a bond has 5-year duration, a 1% rise in rates causes approximately 5% price decline.

| Duration | Rate Change | Price Change |
|----------|-------------|--------------|
| 2 years | +1% | -2% |
| 5 years | +1% | -5% |
| 10 years | +1% | -10% |
| 20 years | +1% | -20% |

Longer-term bonds are more volatile.

### Why Hold Bonds?

1. **Income:** Regular interest payments
2. **Stability:** Lower volatility than stocks
3. **Diversification:** Often move opposite to stocks
4. **Capital preservation:** Return of principal at maturity
5. **Deflation hedge:** Bonds do well when economy struggles

### Bond Risks

- **Interest rate risk:** Prices fall when rates rise
- **Credit/default risk:** Issuer may not pay
- **Inflation risk:** Fixed payments lose purchasing power
- **Call risk:** Issuer may pay off bond early

## Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

ETFs are investment funds that trade on stock exchanges like individual stocks.

### How ETFs Work

An ETF holds a basket of securities (stocks, bonds, commodities, etc.) and trades throughout the day at market-determined prices.

**Example:** SPY (S&P 500 ETF) holds all 500 stocks in the S&P 500 in their proper weights. Buy SPY, and you effectively own 500 stocks in one trade.

### ETF Structure

1. **Authorized Participants (APs):** Large institutions that create/redeem ETF shares
2. **Creation/Redemption:** APs exchange baskets of underlying securities for ETF shares
3. **Arbitrage:** This mechanism keeps ETF price close to its underlying value (NAV)

### ETF vs. Mutual Fund

| Feature | ETF | Mutual Fund |
|---------|-----|-------------|
| Trading | Throughout the day | Once daily (4 PM) |
| Pricing | Real-time market price | End-of-day NAV |
| Minimum Investment | One share | Often $1,000+ |
| Expense Ratios | Generally lower | Generally higher |
| Tax Efficiency | More efficient | Less efficient |
| Automatic Investment | Harder to automate | Easy to automate |

### Types of ETFs

**Index ETFs:** Track an index (most common)
- VOO (S&P 500)
- VTI (Total U.S. Stock Market)
- BND (Total Bond Market)

**Sector ETFs:** Focus on specific industries
- XLK (Technology)
- XLF (Financials)
- XLE (Energy)

**Factor ETFs:** Target specific characteristics
- VTV (Value)
- VUG (Growth)
- MTUM (Momentum)

**Bond ETFs:** Hold fixed income
- BND (Total Bond Market)
- TLT (Long-Term Treasury)
- HYG (High Yield Corporate)

**Commodity ETFs:** Track commodities
- GLD (Gold)
- USO (Oil)
- DBA (Agriculture)

**International ETFs:** Foreign stocks
- VEA (Developed Markets)
- VWO (Emerging Markets)

**Leveraged/Inverse ETFs:** Amplified or opposite returns
- **Warning:** These are for short-term trading only. They decay over time and are not suitable for long-term holding.

### Advantages of ETFs

- **Diversification:** Hundreds/thousands of securities in one trade
- **Low costs:** Many have expense ratios under 0.10%
- **Tax efficiency:** Minimal capital gains distributions
- **Flexibility:** Trade anytime during market hours
- **Transparency:** Holdings disclosed daily

### Disadvantages of ETFs

- **Trading costs:** Bid-ask spread on each trade
- **Premiums/discounts:** Price can deviate from NAV
- **Complexity:** Some specialized ETFs are risky
- **Auto-invest challenges:** Harder than mutual funds for automatic investing

## Mutual Funds

Mutual funds pool money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio of securities.

### How Mutual Funds Work

1. You send money to the fund company
2. Professional managers invest the pooled money
3. You own shares in the fund
4. The fund's NAV (net asset value) is calculated daily at 4 PM
5. All trades execute at that day's NAV

### Types of Mutual Funds

**By Management Style:**

*Index Funds:* Passively track an index
- Low expense ratios (0.03-0.20%)
- Match market returns minus fees
- Example: VFIAX (Vanguard S&P 500)

*Actively Managed:* Fund manager picks investments
- Higher expense ratios (0.50-1.50%+)
- Attempt to beat the market
- Most fail to beat their benchmark over long periods

**By Asset Class:**
- Stock funds
- Bond funds
- Balanced funds (stocks + bonds)
- Money market funds

**By Investment Style:**
- Growth funds
- Value funds
- International funds
- Sector funds

### Share Classes

Mutual funds often have multiple share classes:

| Class | Sales Load | Expense Ratio | Best For |
|-------|------------|---------------|----------|
| A | Front-end (3-5%) | Lower ongoing | Large investments |
| B | Back-end (decreasing) | Higher ongoing | Rarely best choice |
| C | Level load (1%/year) | Highest ongoing | Short-term holding |
| Institutional | None | Lowest | 401k plans |
| No-load | None | Varies | Direct investors |

**Advice:** Avoid funds with sales loads. Plenty of excellent no-load options exist.

### Expense Ratios Matter

**Example:** $100,000 invested for 30 years at 7% return

| Expense Ratio | Final Value | Lost to Fees |
|---------------|-------------|--------------|
| 0.04% | $749,318 | $12,000 |
| 0.50% | $661,437 | $100,000 |
| 1.00% | $574,349 | $187,000 |
| 1.50% | $498,395 | $263,000 |

A 1% difference in fees costs you nearly $200,000 over 30 years.

### Target-Date Funds

All-in-one funds that automatically adjust allocation as you approach retirement.

**Example:** Target 2055 Fund
- Today (30 years to retirement): 90% stocks, 10% bonds
- In 15 years: 75% stocks, 25% bonds
- At retirement: 50% stocks, 50% bonds

**Pros:** Set-it-and-forget-it simplicity
**Cons:** One-size-fits-all may not match your situation; fees on fees

### Do Active Managers Beat the Market?

**Data says:** Rarely, and not consistently.

Over 15-year periods, approximately 90% of actively managed funds underperform their benchmark index after fees. Even funds that outperform rarely do so consistently.

This is why low-cost index funds are recommended for most investors.

## Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)

REITs are companies that own, operate, or finance income-producing real estate.

### How REITs Work

- Must distribute 90%+ of taxable income as dividends
- Traded on exchanges like stocks
- Provide exposure to real estate without buying property

### Types of REITs

**Equity REITs:** Own and operate properties
- Office buildings
- Retail centers
- Apartments
- Warehouses
- Data centers
- Healthcare facilities

**Mortgage REITs (mREITs):** Finance real estate through mortgages
- Higher yields
- Interest rate sensitive
- More volatile

**Hybrid REITs:** Combination of equity and mortgage

### Why Include REITs?

1. **Income:** High dividend yields (3-8% typical)
2. **Diversification:** Different behavior than stocks/bonds
3. **Inflation hedge:** Real estate values often rise with inflation
4. **Liquidity:** Trade like stocks (unlike actual real estate)

### REIT Risks

- **Interest rate sensitivity:** Higher rates hurt valuations
- **Sector concentration:** Some focus on single property types
- **Tax inefficiency:** REIT dividends taxed as ordinary income
- **Economic sensitivity:** Tied to property market conditions

### How to Invest

- Individual REITs: Realty Income (O), Prologis (PLD), Digital Realty (DLR)
- REIT ETFs: VNQ (Vanguard Real Estate), IYR (iShares U.S. Real Estate)

## Money Market Funds

Ultra-safe, liquid investments that hold short-term debt instruments.

### Characteristics

- **Yield:** Tracks short-term interest rates (currently 4-5%)
- **Risk:** Extremely low (but not FDIC insured)
- **NAV:** Typically stable at $1.00/share
- **Liquidity:** Same-day or next-day access

### Types

- **Government:** Treasury bills and government agency debt
- **Prime:** Corporate commercial paper (slightly higher yield, slightly more risk)
- **Tax-exempt:** Municipal short-term debt

### Use Cases

- Emergency fund storage
- Holding cash in brokerage accounts
- Waiting for investment opportunities
- Near-term spending needs

## Building a Portfolio

A well-diversified portfolio typically includes multiple security types:

**Simple Three-Fund Portfolio:**
1. U.S. Stock Index (VTI or VTSAX)
2. International Stock Index (VXUS or VTIAX)
3. Bond Index (BND or VBTLX)

**Example Allocation (30-year-old):**
- 60% U.S. Stocks
- 25% International Stocks
- 15% Bonds

This simple approach captures global equity returns with some bond stability.

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Stocks** offer growth but with volatility
- **Bonds** provide income and stability; prices move opposite to interest rates
- **ETFs** offer diversified, low-cost, tax-efficient exposure to any asset class
- **Mutual funds** are similar but trade once daily; avoid high fees
- **REITs** provide real estate exposure with stock-like liquidity
- **Expense ratios matter** - small fee differences compound into huge amounts
- **Most investors are well-served by simple index funds**
