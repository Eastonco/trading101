# Chapter 55: Cognitive Biases in Trading

## Overview

Your brain is not designed for trading. It evolved to keep you alive on the savanna, not to navigate financial markets. The mental shortcuts that helped our ancestors survive—quick pattern recognition, loss aversion, following the crowd—work against us when investing. This chapter examines the most damaging cognitive biases in trading and provides practical strategies to overcome them.

---

## What Are Cognitive Biases?

### Definition

**Cognitive biases are systematic patterns of deviation from rationality.** They're mental shortcuts (heuristics) that sometimes lead to errors in judgment.

### Why They're Dangerous in Trading

**Markets exploit these biases:**
- Prices often move opposite to what feels right
- The crowd is usually wrong at extremes
- Our instincts push us to buy high and sell low

**You can know about biases and still fall victim to them.** Awareness is necessary but not sufficient—you need systems to counteract them.

---

## Confirmation Bias

### What It Is

**The tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information that confirms your existing beliefs.**

### How It Manifests in Trading

**Seeking confirming information:**
- You're bullish on a stock, so you read only bullish articles
- You ignore bearish analysis or dismiss it as "wrong"
- You follow only traders who share your view

**Interpreting ambiguous information as confirming:**
- Neutral news becomes "bullish" because you're long
- Mixed earnings report seen as positive (because you hold)
- You find reasons why bad news doesn't matter

**Remembering selectively:**
- You remember the times your thesis was right
- You forget or minimize the times you were wrong
- Your track record seems better than it is

### Real-World Example

**Trader long NVDA at $800:**
- Reads every positive AI article
- Dismisses competition concerns as "FUD"
- Interprets any dip as "buying opportunity"
- Ignores valuation warnings as "they don't get it"
- Stock drops to $600—shocked, didn't see it coming

### How to Counter It

**Actively seek disconfirming evidence:**
- Before entering any trade, write 3 reasons it could fail
- Read bearish analysis on your bullish positions
- Follow traders with opposing views

**Pre-mortem analysis:**
- Imagine the trade failed. Why did it fail?
- What would need to be true for your thesis to be wrong?
- What would make you change your mind?

**Devil's advocate:**
- Present the best case against your trade
- Could you argue the other side convincingly?

## Loss Aversion

### What It Is

**Losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good.**

A $1,000 loss hurts more than a $1,000 gain pleases. This asymmetry leads to irrational decision-making.

### How It Manifests in Trading

**Holding losers too long:**
- "I can't sell now—I'd lock in a loss"
- "It'll come back"
- "I'm not selling at a loss"

**Selling winners too early:**
- "I should take this profit before it disappears"
- "A bird in hand..."
- Lock in small gains, let small losses become large

**Result:** The opposite of what works. Winners get cut short, losers run.

### The Math Problem

**With a 50% win rate:**
- If average winner = $500 and average loser = $500: Breakeven
- If average winner = $300 (cut early) and loser = $700 (held): Losing money
- Even 60% win rate loses with poor risk/reward

### Real-World Example

**Portfolio of two positions:**
- Stock A: Up 30% ($3,000 gain on $10,000 position)
- Stock B: Down 30% ($3,000 loss on $10,000 position)

**What loss aversion makes you do:**
- Sell Stock A ("lock in the gain!")
- Hold Stock B ("can't sell at a loss!")

**What you should ask:**
- Which position has better prospects going forward?
- Forget your entry price—which would you buy today?

### How to Counter It

**Pre-set stop losses:**
- Define your exit before entering
- Use automatic stop-loss orders
- Accept the loss as cost of doing business

**Think in terms of expected value, not feelings:**
- Would I enter this position today at this price?
- What is the probability-weighted outcome?

**Reframe losses:**
- "I'm not losing—I'm paying tuition"
- "Small losses prevent big losses"
- "This is the cost of being in the game"

**The "new money" exercise:**
- Imagine your position was cash
- Would you buy this stock at this price today?
- If not, sell. Your entry price is irrelevant.

## Recency Bias

### What It Is

**Overweighting recent events and underweighting historical data.**

What happened last week feels more relevant than what happened last year.

### How It Manifests in Trading

**After market goes up:**
- "Stocks always go up"
- More aggressive positioning
- Ignore historical crashes

**After market goes down:**
- "Markets are too risky"
- Excessive caution
- Forget that recoveries always come

**After recent winning streak:**
- "I've figured this out"
- Increase position sizes
- Abandon risk management

**After recent losing streak:**
- "This strategy doesn't work"
- Abandon proven approach
- Chase different strategy

### Real-World Example

**March 2020:**
- Fastest 30%+ crash in history
- Felt like the world was ending
- Many sold at the bottom

**March 2020 to December 2020:**
- Market rallied 70%+
- Those who sold missed it
- Recency bias (fear) caused real losses

**December 2021:**
- Everything going up
- "Stocks only go up"
- Maximum bullishness at the top

**2022:**
- Market dropped 25%+
- Those who bought aggressively at top devastated
- Recency bias (greed) caused real losses

### How to Counter It

**Study market history:**
- Markets have always recovered from crashes
- Markets have always had crashes
- Both are true

**Use base rates:**
- What's the historical probability of this event?
- How often does this pattern actually work?
- What does long-term data say?

**Wait before reacting:**
- Recent events feel more important than they are
- Give yourself 24-48 hours before changing strategy
- Ask: "Would I think differently about this in a month?"

**Keep a long-term journal:**
- Record how you felt at past extremes
- Review during future extremes
- Your past self can guide your present self

## Anchoring Bias

### What It Is

**Fixating on a reference point (anchor) and making decisions relative to it, even when the anchor is irrelevant.**

### How It Manifests in Trading

**Anchoring to entry price:**
- "I can't sell until I'm back to breakeven"
- Entry price is irrelevant to future prospects
- The market doesn't know or care what you paid

**Anchoring to historical highs:**
- "NVDA hit $500, now it's $300—it's cheap!"
- Previous high is irrelevant to fair value
- Stock could be expensive at $300 if fundamentals changed

**Anchoring to arbitrary targets:**
- "I'll sell at $100" (round number)
- "I'll sell when I double my money"
- Why $100? Why not $95 or $105?

### Real-World Example

**Trader buys AAPL at $180:**
- Stock drops to $150
- "I'll sell when it gets back to $180"
- Stock recovers to $175, then drops to $140
- Still holding, waiting for $180
- Meanwhile, $175 was a great exit

**The anchor ($180) prevented a good decision.**

### How to Counter It

**Focus on current value, not entry price:**
- Would I buy this stock at today's price?
- What is the stock worth based on fundamentals?
- Where would a new analyst set the target?

**Use multiple reference points:**
- Don't rely on one anchor
- Consider various valuation methods
- Look at different timeframe charts

**Hide your entry price:**
- Some traders hide cost basis from display
- Forces decisions based on current reality
- Your P&L shouldn't affect your analysis

**Ask: "What would I do if I just inherited this position?"**
- Remove emotional attachment to entry
- Evaluate purely on forward prospects

## Overconfidence Bias

### What It Is

**Overestimating your knowledge, abilities, and the precision of your predictions.**

### How It Manifests in Trading

**After winning trades:**
- "I'm good at this"
- Attribute success to skill, not luck
- Increase risk taking

**Illusion of control:**
- "I can time the market"
- "I would have sold before the crash"
- "I know what will happen"

**Excessive trading:**
- More confident = more trades
- Research shows overconfident traders trade more and earn less

**Underestimating risks:**
- "This is a sure thing"
- "It can't go lower"
- Famous last words before blowups

### The Dunning-Kruger Connection

**Beginners often most overconfident:**
- Don't know what they don't know
- Early luck feels like skill
- Haven't experienced a real drawdown

**Experienced traders often more humble:**
- Have been humbled by the market
- Know how unpredictable markets are
- Respect the possibility of being wrong

### Real-World Example

**New trader in 2020-2021:**
- Started trading during bull market
- Everything they bought went up
- "I'm a natural at this"
- Increased position sizes
- Used margin and options
- 2022: Portfolio devastated
- "The market is rigged" (blame externally)

### How to Counter It

**Track your actual results:**
- Not your remembered results—your actual results
- Include all trades, not just winners
- Calculate your real win rate and expectancy

**Compare to benchmarks:**
- Are you beating the S&P 500?
- After fees and taxes?
- Over a meaningful time period (3+ years)?

**Practice probabilistic thinking:**
- Never say "I know"—say "I think there's a 60% chance"
- Assign confidence levels to predictions
- Track accuracy over time

**Remember your failures:**
- Keep a "humility file" of your worst trades
- Review before making large bets
- Past mistakes are valuable teachers

**Assume you could be wrong:**
- Size positions assuming you might be wrong
- Use stop losses
- Never bet the farm

## Gambler's Fallacy

### What It Is

**The belief that past random events affect future probabilities.**

"The coin has landed heads 5 times—tails is due."

### How It Manifests in Trading

**After consecutive down days:**
- "It has to bounce—we're oversold"
- "It can't go down forever"
- Markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent

**After consecutive losses:**
- "My next trade has to win"
- "I'm due for a winner"
- Each trade is independent

**Counter-trend trading without edge:**
- "This rally is extended—I'll short"
- "This drop is overdone—I'll buy"
- Without a real edge, you're just gambling

### The Reality

**Each event is independent (mostly):**
- Past coin flips don't affect future flips
- Past price moves don't guarantee future moves
- Markets have no memory of your trades

**What IS true:**
- Mean reversion exists over longer timeframes
- But timing is nearly impossible
- "The market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent"

### Real-World Example

**Stock down 5 days in a row:**
- Gambler's fallacy: "It's due for an up day"
- Reality: Could go down 5 more days
- Some of the worst crashes came after "oversold" conditions
- March 2020: "Oversold" at -20%, then dropped another -15%

### How to Counter It

**Each trade stands alone:**
- Past results don't affect probability of next trade
- Don't increase size because "you're due"
- Don't skip good setups because "you've had enough winners"

**Focus on edge, not luck:**
- Does this setup have a statistical edge?
- What's the base rate for this pattern?
- Are you trading a system or feelings?

**Accept randomness:**
- Short-term results are noisy
- You can do everything right and lose
- Judge by process, not outcomes

## Sunk Cost Fallacy

### What It Is

**Continuing a course of action because of previously invested resources (time, money, effort), even when it no longer makes sense.**

### How It Manifests in Trading

**Holding losing positions:**
- "I've already lost $5,000—I can't sell now"
- "I've held this for two years—I can't give up"
- Past investment is irrelevant to future prospects

**Averaging down without a plan:**
- "I've bought so much, might as well buy more"
- "I need to lower my average cost"
- Throwing good money after bad

**Staying in a bad strategy:**
- "I've spent six months learning this system"
- "I've invested so much time"
- Past time investment shouldn't drive future decisions

### Real-World Example

**Trader holds a losing stock:**
- Bought at $100
- Now $60 (down 40%)
- "I can't sell at this loss after holding for a year"
- Stock drops to $30
- "Now I really can't sell"
- Stock drops to $15
- "I'll just hold forever"

**Total loss: 85%—all because they couldn't accept a 40% loss.**

### How to Counter It

**Focus only on future expectations:**
- What are the prospects from here?
- Ignore what you've invested
- Past costs are sunk—gone forever

**Ask the "clean slate" question:**
- If I had cash instead of this position, would I buy it today?
- If no, sell
- Your entry price and holding period are irrelevant

**Set rules in advance:**
- "I will sell if the stock drops 20%"
- "I will sell if the thesis breaks"
- Follow rules, not feelings

**Mental write-off:**
- Consider the money already gone
- Now make the best decision for the money that remains
- Don't throw more at a lost cause

## Herding (Social Proof) Bias

### What It Is

**The tendency to follow the crowd, assuming others know something you don't.**

### How It Manifests in Trading

**FOMO (Fear of Missing Out):**
- Everyone's buying—must be a good idea
- Don't want to miss the gains
- Chase into overextended moves

**Capitulation:**
- Everyone's selling—I should too
- Panic selling at the bottom
- "If smart money is selling..."

**Following gurus:**
- "This famous investor bought it"
- "This Twitter trader is up 10,000%"
- Blindly following others' trades

### Why Herding Fails

**Crowd is often wrong at extremes:**
- Maximum bullishness = market tops
- Maximum bearishness = market bottoms
- Being contrarian is hard but often profitable

**By the time you hear about it, it's usually late:**
- Retail learns about trends last
- Smart money already positioned
- You're buying their exit liquidity

### Real-World Example

**GameStop (GME) January 2021:**
- Stock went from $20 to $483
- Retail FOMO at extreme levels
- Everyone talking about it
- Those who bought near top: -90%+ losses

**Bitcoin at $69,000 (November 2021):**
- "Everyone" buying crypto
- Maximum hype and adoption
- "It's going to $100,000"
- Followed by -77% crash

### How to Counter It

**Be wary of consensus:**
- When everyone agrees, be skeptical
- Extreme sentiment often marks turns
- "When the shoeshine boy gives stock tips..."

**Do your own analysis:**
- Why specifically is this a good trade?
- Would you trade it if no one else was?
- Can you articulate the thesis?

**Measure sentiment:**
- Track bullish/bearish surveys
- Monitor put/call ratios
- Extreme readings are warnings

**Slow down:**
- FOMO creates urgency
- Good trades are patient
- If you must buy NOW, you're probably too late

## Hindsight Bias

### What It Is

**After an event, believing you "knew it all along."**

Also called the "I knew it" effect.

### How It Manifests in Trading

**After market moves:**
- "I knew the market would crash"
- "I knew that stock would double"
- But you didn't actually trade it

**Overconfidence in predictions:**
- Past "predictions" seem obvious
- Future predictions feel equally obvious
- Creates false confidence

**Failure to learn:**
- If you "knew all along," nothing to learn
- Misremember past uncertainty
- Don't improve decision process

### Real-World Example

**March 2020 COVID crash:**

**In January 2020:**
- Few predicted pandemic crash
- Market at all-time highs
- Analysts bullish

**In April 2020:**
- "Anyone could see this coming"
- "The warning signs were obvious"
- "I knew we'd have a crash"

**Reality:** Almost no one sold before the crash. If they "knew," why didn't they act?

### How to Counter It

**Keep a decision journal:**
- Write predictions BEFORE events
- Record reasoning at the time
- Review with actual outcomes

**Record uncertainty:**
- Note your confidence level at the time
- "I'm 60% confident the market will rise"
- Compare to actual outcomes

**Be honest with yourself:**
- Did you actually predict it?
- Did you act on the prediction?
- If not, you didn't really know

**Focus on process, not outcome:**
- Was the decision reasonable given information at the time?
- Don't judge past decisions with future information
- Would the same process usually work?

## Practical Framework: Bias Audit

### Weekly Review Questions

**Confirmation bias:**
- Did I seek out opposing views?
- What would disconfirm my thesis?

**Loss aversion:**
- Am I holding any losers I should cut?
- Did I sell any winners too early?

**Recency bias:**
- Am I overreacting to recent events?
- What does long-term data say?

**Anchoring:**
- Am I fixated on any meaningless numbers?
- Would I buy these positions at today's prices?

**Overconfidence:**
- How certain am I? Is that warranted?
- What could I be wrong about?

**Herding:**
- Am I trading because others are?
- Is this my own analysis or following?

### Pre-Trade Checklist

Before every trade, ask:
- [ ] What would make me wrong?
- [ ] Am I chasing performance?
- [ ] Is this my own analysis?
- [ ] Am I trying to win back losses?
- [ ] What's my actual confidence level?
- [ ] Would I take this trade with no position?

## Key Takeaways

- **Cognitive biases are universal** - Everyone has them; awareness is the first step
- **Confirmation bias** - Actively seek disconfirming evidence for your positions
- **Loss aversion** - Pre-set stops; losses are tuition, not personal failure
- **Recency bias** - Recent events aren't more important; study long-term history
- **Anchoring** - Your entry price is irrelevant to future value; ignore it
- **Overconfidence** - Track actual results; assume you could be wrong
- **Gambler's fallacy** - Each trade is independent; you're not "due" for anything
- **Sunk cost fallacy** - Past investments don't justify future ones; cut losers
- **Herding** - Be wary of consensus; do your own analysis
- **Systems beat willpower** - Build processes that counteract biases automatically
