# Chapter 48: Social Security

## Overview

Social Security will likely be your largest source of guaranteed retirement income. Understanding how benefits are calculated, when to claim, and strategies for maximizing benefits can mean tens of thousands of dollars in additional lifetime income. This chapter demystifies Social Security and helps you make informed decisions.

---

## How Social Security Works

### The Basics

**Funding:** Payroll taxes (FICA) - 6.2% from you, 6.2% from employer
**Eligibility:** 40 credits (roughly 10 years of work)
**Benefits:** Monthly payments for life, adjusted for inflation

### How Benefits Are Calculated

**Step 1: Calculate AIME (Average Indexed Monthly Earnings)**
- Take your 35 highest-earning years
- Adjust past earnings for wage inflation
- Calculate average monthly earnings

**Step 2: Apply Benefit Formula (2024)**
- 90% of first $1,174 of AIME
- 32% of AIME between $1,174 and $7,078
- 15% of AIME above $7,078

**Result:** Primary Insurance Amount (PIA) - Your benefit at Full Retirement Age

### Example Calculation

**Worker with $60,000 average annual earnings:**
- AIME: $5,000/month
- Benefit: 90% × $1,174 = $1,057
- Plus: 32% × ($5,000 - $1,174) = $1,224
- **PIA: $2,281/month**

### Full Retirement Age (FRA)

| Birth Year | Full Retirement Age |
|------------|---------------------|
| 1954 or earlier | 66 |
| 1955 | 66 + 2 months |
| 1956 | 66 + 4 months |
| 1957 | 66 + 6 months |
| 1958 | 66 + 8 months |
| 1959 | 66 + 10 months |
| 1960 or later | 67 |

## When to Claim

### Claiming Options

**Age 62:** Earliest possible (reduced benefits)
**Full Retirement Age:** 100% of PIA
**Age 70:** Maximum benefits (delayed credits stop)

### Early vs. Late Claiming

| Claiming Age | % of PIA (FRA 67) | Monthly (PIA $2,000) |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| 62 | 70% | $1,400 |
| 63 | 75% | $1,500 |
| 64 | 80% | $1,600 |
| 65 | 86.7% | $1,734 |
| 66 | 93.3% | $1,866 |
| 67 (FRA) | 100% | $2,000 |
| 68 | 108% | $2,160 |
| 69 | 116% | $2,320 |
| 70 | 124% | $2,480 |

### The Math of Delaying

**Monthly benefit increases:**
- Before FRA: ~6.67% per year penalty for early claiming
- After FRA: 8% per year increase for delaying

**Break-even analysis:**
- Claiming at 62 vs. 67: Break-even ~80
- Claiming at 67 vs. 70: Break-even ~82

**If you live past break-even age, delaying wins.**

### Factors in Claiming Decision

**Consider claiming early (62-64) if:**
- Poor health/shorter life expectancy
- Need the income immediately
- Can invest benefits at higher return (rare)
- Spouse can delay (maximize one benefit)

**Consider waiting until 70 if:**
- Good health/longevity
- Still working (benefits reduced anyway)
- Spouse relies on your record for survivor benefit
- Other income can bridge the gap
- Want maximum guaranteed income

## Working While Receiving Benefits

### Earnings Test (Before FRA)

**If you claim early and still work:**
- In years before FRA: $1 withheld for every $2 earned above $22,320 (2024)
- In year you reach FRA: $1 withheld for every $3 earned above $59,520

**After FRA:** No earnings limit

### Withheld Benefits Aren't Lost

**Good news:** Benefits withheld are added back to your benefit at FRA.

**Example:**
- Claim at 62, benefits reduced 30%
- Earn too much, $6,000 withheld over 3 years
- At FRA, benefit is recalculated as if you claimed later
- Results in slightly higher benefit going forward

## Spousal Benefits

### Spousal Benefit Basics

**Married?** You can claim on your own record OR up to 50% of spouse's PIA.

**Eligibility:**
- Married at least 1 year
- Spouse must have filed for their benefit

### Spousal Benefit Amount

**At your FRA:** Up to 50% of spouse's PIA
**Before FRA:** Reduced for early claiming
**No delayed credits:** Spousal benefit doesn't increase past FRA

### Example

**Higher earner PIA:** $2,500
**Lower earner PIA:** $1,000
**Lower earner spousal benefit:** $1,250 (50% of $2,500)

**Lower earner receives $1,250** (higher of own benefit or spousal).

### Divorced Spousal Benefits

**Eligible if:**
- Married at least 10 years
- Divorced at least 2 years
- Not remarried
- Ex-spouse eligible for benefits

**Benefit:** Up to 50% of ex-spouse's PIA (doesn't affect their benefit)

## Survivor Benefits

### What Survivors Receive

**When a spouse dies:**
- Surviving spouse can receive up to 100% of deceased's benefit
- Can switch between own benefit and survivor benefit
- Subject to age reductions if claimed early

### Survivor Benefit Amount

| Claiming Age | % of Deceased's Benefit |
|--------------|------------------------|
| 60 | 71.5% |
| 62 | 81% |
| 64 | 90.5% |
| FRA | 100% |

### Survivor Claiming Strategy

**If your benefit is smaller:**
- Take own benefit early
- Switch to survivor benefit at FRA (100%)

**If your benefit is larger:**
- Take survivor benefit early
- Switch to own benefit at 70 (124%)

### Why This Matters for Delaying

**If higher earner delays until 70:**
- Gets 124% of PIA while alive
- Surviving spouse gets 124% of PIA after death
- Permanent increase in survivor benefit

## Social Security and Taxes

### When Benefits Are Taxable

**Your "combined income" determines taxation:**
Combined Income = AGI + Tax-Exempt Interest + 50% of Social Security

| Filing Status | Combined Income | Benefits Taxable |
|---------------|-----------------|------------------|
| Single | Below $25,000 | 0% |
| Single | $25,000-34,000 | Up to 50% |
| Single | Above $34,000 | Up to 85% |
| Married | Below $32,000 | 0% |
| Married | $32,000-44,000 | Up to 50% |
| Married | Above $44,000 | Up to 85% |

**Note:** Up to 85% of benefits can be taxed, not 85% tax rate.

### Tax Planning Strategies

**Before claiming:**
- Do Roth conversions while in lower bracket
- Deplete traditional accounts partially
- Reduce future taxable income

**In retirement:**
- Manage withdrawals to minimize benefit taxation
- Consider timing of Roth conversions
- Use tax-free accounts strategically

## Social Security's Future

### The Trust Fund Issue

**Current status:**
- Benefits exceed tax revenue
- Trust fund covers shortfall
- Trust fund projected to deplete around 2033-2034

**After depletion:**
- Benefits would be cut to ~77% of scheduled amounts
- Without Congressional action

### Will Benefits Be Cut?

**Political reality:**
- Social Security is politically popular
- Changes likely before depletion
- Options: Raise taxes, reduce benefits, raise retirement age, or combination

**Planning implication:**
- Don't count on 100% of projected benefits
- Build personal savings as primary retirement source
- Social Security as supplement, not foundation

## Maximizing Your Benefits

### Strategy 1: Work 35 Years

**Why:** Fewer than 35 years = zeros averaged in

**Example:**
- 30 years of $50,000 average = $50,000 AIME basis
- Plus 5 years of $0 = Reduces AIME significantly

### Strategy 2: Increase Earnings in Peak Years

**Why:** 35 highest years count

**If you're in your 50s-60s:**
- Higher earnings now replace lower earlier years
- Can significantly boost benefits

### Strategy 3: Delay Claiming (If Possible)

**Why:** 8% per year guaranteed return

**Best for:** Healthy individuals, higher earners, those with sufficient bridge income

### Strategy 4: Coordinate with Spouse

**Strategies:**
- Higher earner delays to 70 (maximizes survivor benefit)
- Lower earner claims early (bridge income)
- Consider spousal benefit timing

### Strategy 5: Check Your Earnings Record

**Why:** Errors happen

**How:** Create my Social Security account at ssa.gov
**When:** Check annually

## Key Takeaways

- **35 highest years of earnings determine benefits** - Work history matters
- **Full Retirement Age is 66-67** - Depends on birth year
- **Delayed credits add 8% per year** - Guaranteed return through age 70
- **Break-even is roughly 80-82** - Consider life expectancy
- **Spousal benefits can reach 50% of higher earner's PIA**
- **Survivor benefits can reach 100%** - Higher earner delaying helps surviving spouse
- **Up to 85% of benefits can be taxed** - Plan for this
- **Trust fund issues are real** - But benefits unlikely to disappear
